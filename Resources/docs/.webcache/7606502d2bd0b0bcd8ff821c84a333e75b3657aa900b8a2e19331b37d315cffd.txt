A **solid-state drive** (**SSD**) is a type of [solid-state storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid-state_storage "Solid-state storage") device that uses [integrated circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_circuit "Integrated circuit") to store data [persistently](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persistence_(computer_science) "Persistence (computer science)"). It is sometimes called **semiconductor storage device**, **solid-state device**, or **solid-state disk**.^([\[1\]](#cite_note-iBgF2-1)[\[2\]](#cite_note-STEC-2)) Etymologically, "solid-state drive" partly refers to existing data storage devices like [tape drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tape_drives "Tape drives") and [hard disk drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_drives "Hard disk drives"), which all use a mechanically driven storage medium.

SSDs rely on non-volatile memory, typically [NAND flash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAND_flash "NAND flash"), to store data in memory cells. The performance and endurance of SSDs vary depending on the number of bits stored per cell, ranging from high-performing single-level cells (SLC) to more affordable but slower quad-level cells (QLC). In addition to flash-based SSDs, other technologies such as [3D XPoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_XPoint "3D XPoint") offer faster speeds and higher endurance through different data storage mechanisms.

Unlike traditional [hard disk drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_drive "Hard disk drive") (HDDs), SSDs have no moving parts, allowing them to deliver faster data access speeds, reduced latency, increased resistance to physical shock, lower power consumption, and silent operation.

Often interfaced to a system in the same way as HDDs, SSDs are used in a variety of devices, including [personal computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_computer "Personal computer"), [enterprise servers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Server_(computing) "Server (computing)"), and [mobile devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_device "Mobile device"). However, SSDs are generally more expensive on a per-gigabyte basis and have a finite number of write cycles, which can lead to data loss over time. Despite these limitations, SSDs are increasingly replacing HDDs, especially in performance-critical applications and as primary storage in many consumer devices.

SSDs come in various form factors and interface types, including [SATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SATA "SATA"), [PCIe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PCIe "PCIe"), and [NVMe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NVMe "NVMe"), each offering different levels of performance. Hybrid storage solutions, such as [solid-state hybrid drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybrid_drive "Hybrid drive") (SSHDs), combine SSD and HDD technologies to offer improved performance at a lower cost than pure SSDs.

## Attributes

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Attributes")\]

An SSD stores data in [semiconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor "Semiconductor") cells, with its properties varying according to the number of [bits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit_(computing) "Bit (computing)") stored in each cell (between 1 and 4). Single-level cells (SLC) store one bit of data per cell and provide higher performance and endurance. In contrast, multi-level cells (MLC), triple-level cells (TLC), and quad-level cells (QLC) store more data per cell but have lower performance and endurance. SSDs using [3D XPoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_XPoint "3D XPoint") technology, such as Intel's Optane, store data by changing electrical resistance instead of storing electrical charges in cells, which can provide faster speeds and longer data persistence compared to conventional flash memory.^([\[3\]](#cite_note-SNIA-101-3)) SSDs based on [NAND flash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAND_flash "NAND flash") slowly leak charge when not powered, while heavily used consumer drives may start losing data typically after one to two years unpowered in storage.^([\[4\]](#cite_note-Vättö-4)) SSDs have a limited lifetime number of writes, and also slow down as they reach their full storage capacity.^(\[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*\])

SSDs also have internal parallelism that allows them to manage multiple operations simultaneously, which enhances their performance.^([\[5\]](#cite_note-5))

Unlike HDDs and similar [electromechanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromechanical "Electromechanical") [magnetic storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_storage "Magnetic storage"), SSDs do not have moving mechanical parts, which provides advantages such as resistance to physical shock, quieter operation, and faster access times. Their lower latency results in higher input/output rates (IOPS) than HDDs.^([\[6\]](#cite_note-dell-study-6))

Some SSDs are combined with traditional hard drives in hybrid configurations, such as Intel's [Hystor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hystor?action=edit&redlink=1 "Hystor (page does not exist)") and Apple's [Fusion Drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fusion_Drive "Fusion Drive"). These drives use both flash memory and spinning magnetic disks in order to improve the performance of frequently accessed data.^([\[7\]](#cite_note-Hystor-7)[\[8\]](#cite_note-WD_Black_SSHD_-_CNET_Reviews-8))

Traditional interfaces (e.g. [SATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SATA "SATA") and [SAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_Attached_SCSI "Serial Attached SCSI")) and [standard HDD form factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_disk_drive_form_factors "List of disk drive form factors") allow such SSDs to be used as drop-in replacements for HDDs in computers and other devices. Newer form factors such as [mSATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MSATA "MSATA"), [M.2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M.2 "M.2"), [U.2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.2 "U.2"), [NF1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samsung_NF1 "Samsung NF1")/[M.3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M.3_(computing) "M.3 (computing)")/[NGFF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NGFF "NGFF"),^([\[9\]](#cite_note-RLVlL-9)[\[10\]](#cite_note-XSOC1-10)) [XFM Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XFM_Express "XFM Express") (*[Crossover Flash Memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossover_Flash_Memory "Crossover Flash Memory")*, form factor XT2)^([\[11\]](#cite_note-Q6pr6-11)) and [EDSFF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enterprise_and_Data_Center_Standard_Form_Factor "Enterprise and Data Center Standard Form Factor")^([\[12\]](#cite_note-mhkTi-12)[\[13\]](#cite_note-SKezS-13)) and higher speed interfaces such as [NVM Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NVM_Express "NVM Express") (NVMe) over [PCI Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PCI_Express "PCI Express") (PCIe) can further increase performance over HDD performance.^([\[3\]](#cite_note-SNIA-101-3))

#### Hard disk drives

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Hard disk drives")\]

See also: [Hard disk drive performance characteristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_drive_performance_characteristics "Hard disk drive performance characteristics")

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a8/480_GB_OCZ-AGIL_ITY3.png/500px-480_GB_OCZ-AGIL_ITY3.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:480_GB_OCZ-AGIL_ITY3.png)

Benchmark of a 500GB SATA-2 SSD ([OCZ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OCZ "OCZ") Agility-3, circa 2011), showing about 230 MB/s reading speed (blue), 210 MB/s writing speed (red) and about 0.1 ms seek time (green), all independent from the accessed disk location. As of 2026, Gen5 SSDs can reach up to 15GB/s, a 60x improvement over this SSD.

Traditional HDD [benchmarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benchmark_(computing) "Benchmark (computing)") tend to focus on the performance characteristics such as [rotational latency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotational_latency "Rotational latency") and [seek time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seek_time "Seek time"). As SSDs do not need to spin or seek to locate data, they are vastly superior to HDDs in such tests. However, SSDs have challenges with mixed reads and writes, and their performance may degrade over time. Therefore, SSD testing typically looks at when the full drive is first used, as the new and empty drive may have much better write performance than it would show after only weeks of use.^([\[14\]](#cite_note-gyMLY-14))

The reliability of both HDDs and SSDs varies greatly among models.^([\[15\]](#cite_note-PC_World_2014-15)) Some field failure rates indicate that SSDs are significantly more reliable than HDDs.^([\[16\]](#cite_note-D5XVI-16)[\[17\]](#cite_note-behardware_2012-17)) However, SSDs are sensitive to sudden power interruption, sometimes resulting in aborted writes or even cases of the complete loss of the drive.^([\[18\]](#cite_note-MSkvR-18))

Most of the advantages of solid-state drives over traditional hard drives are due to their ability to access data completely electronically instead of electromechanically, resulting in superior transfer speeds and mechanical ruggedness.^([\[19\]](#cite_note-jnZgW-19)) On the other hand, hard disk drives offer significantly higher capacity for their price.^([\[6\]](#cite_note-dell-study-6)[\[20\]](#cite_note-QSE1g-20))

In traditional HDDs, a rewritten file will generally occupy the same location on the disk surface as the original file, whereas in SSDs the new copy will often be written to different NAND cells for the purpose of [wear leveling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wear_leveling "Wear leveling"). The wear-leveling algorithms are complex and difficult to test exhaustively. As a result, one major cause of data loss in SSDs is firmware bugs.^([\[21\]](#cite_note-LJEJb-21)[\[22\]](#cite_note-C5LuO-22))

#### Memory cards

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Memory cards")\]

Main article: [Memory card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_card "Memory card")

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f7/CompactFlash_IDE_Adaptor.jpeg/250px-CompactFlash_IDE_Adaptor.jpeg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:CompactFlash_IDE_Adaptor.jpeg)

CompactFlash card used as an SSD

While both [memory cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_card "Memory card") and most SSDs use flash memory, they have very different characteristics, including power consumption, performance, size, and reliability. Originally, solid state drives were shaped and mounted in the computer like hard drives. In contrast, memory cards (such as [Secure Digital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secure_Digital "Secure Digital") (SD), [CompactFlash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CompactFlash "CompactFlash") (CF), and many others) were originally designed for digital cameras and later found their way into cell phones, gaming devices, GPS units, etc. Most memory cards are physically smaller than SSDs, and designed to be inserted and removed repeatedly.^([\[41\]](#cite_note-SanDisk_SSD_CF-41))

### Failure and recovery

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Failure and recovery")\]

SSDs have different [failure modes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Failure_mode "Failure mode") from traditional magnetic hard drives. Because solid-state drives contain no moving parts, they are generally not subject to mechanical failures. However, other types of failures can occur. For example, incomplete or failed writes due to sudden power loss may be more problematic than with HDDs, and the failure of a single chip may result in the loss of all data stored on it. Nonetheless, studies indicate that SSDs are generally reliable, often exceeding their manufacturer-stated lifespan^([\[42\]](#cite_note-2016study-42)[\[43\]](#cite_note-TR2015-43)) and have lower failure rates than HDDs.^([\[42\]](#cite_note-2016study-42)) However, studies also note that SSDs experience higher rates of uncorrectable errors, which can lead to data loss, compared to HDDs.^([\[44\]](#cite_note-u3a39-44))

The endurance of an SSD is typically listed on its datasheet in one of two forms:

- either **n DW/D** (*n drive writes per day*)
- or **m TBW** (*maximum terabytes written*), abbreviated **TBW**.^([\[45\]](#cite_note-WD_doc-45)[\[46\]](#cite_note-46)) For example, a *Samsung 970 EVO NVMe M.2* SSD (2018) with 1 TB of capacity has an endurance rating of 600 TBW.^([\[47\]](#cite_note-Samsung_Evo_970-47))

The basic parameter for determining the lifespan of Flash-NAND systems is the number of programming-erase (P/E) cycles. The TBW parameter is the product of the P/E cycle resource and the SSD capacity. For an SSD with a capacity of 1 TB and TBW = 600 TB, the number of P/E cycles is 600.^([\[48\]](#cite_note-Why_SSD_fails?-48))

Recovering data from SSDs presents challenges due to the non-linear and complex nature of data storage in solid-state drives. The internal operations of SSDs vary by manufacturer, with commands (e.g. [TRIM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trim_(computing) "Trim (computing)") and the ATA Secure Erase) and programs (e.g. [hdparm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hdparm "Hdparm")) being able to erase and modify the bits of a deleted file.

### Reliability metrics

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Reliability metrics")\]

The *[JEDEC Solid State Technology Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JEDEC_Solid_State_Technology_Association "JEDEC Solid State Technology Association")* (JEDEC) has established standards for SSD reliability metrics, which include:^([\[49\]](#cite_note-NullLobur2014-49))

- Unrecoverable Bit Error Ratio (UBER)
- Terabytes Written (TBW) – the total number of terabytes that can be written to a drive within its warranty period
- Drive Writes Per Day (DWPD) – the number of times the full capacity of the drive can be written to per day within its warranty period

### Applications

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Applications")\]

In a [distributed computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_computing "Distributed computing") environment, SSDs can be used as a [distributed cache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_cache "Distributed cache") layer that temporarily absorbs the large volume of user requests to slower HDD-based backend storage systems. This layer provides much higher bandwidth and lower latency than the storage system would, and can be managed in a number of forms, such as a distributed [key-value database](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key-value_database "Key-value database") and a distributed [file system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File_system "File system"). On supercomputers, this layer is typically referred to as [burst buffer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burst_buffer "Burst buffer").

Flash-based solid-state drives can be used to create network appliances from general-purpose [personal computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_computer "Personal computer") hardware. A [write protected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Write_protection "Write protection") flash drive containing the operating system and application software can substitute for larger, less reliable disk drives or CD-ROMs. Appliances built this way can provide an inexpensive alternative to expensive router and firewall hardware.^(\[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*\])

SSDs based on an [SD card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SD_card "SD card") with a [live SD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_SD?action=edit&redlink=1 "Live SD (page does not exist)") operating system are easily [write-locked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SD_card#Optional_write-protect_tab "SD card"). Combined with a [cloud computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_computing "Cloud computing") environment or other writable medium, an [OS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operating_system "Operating system") [booted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booting "Booting") from a write-locked SD card is reliable, persistent and impervious to permanent corruption.

### Hard-drive cache

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Hard-drive cache")\]

In 2011, Intel introduced a caching mechanism for their [Z68](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z68 "Z68") chipset (and mobile derivatives) called [Smart Response Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smart_Response_Technology "Smart Response Technology"), which allows a [SATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SATA "SATA") SSD to be used as a [cache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cache_(computing) "Cache (computing)") (configurable as write-through or [write-back](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Write-back "Write-back")) for a conventional, magnetic hard disk drive.^([\[50\]](#cite_note-pqCuR-50)) A similar technology is available on HighPoint's RocketHybrid [PCIe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PCIe "PCIe") card.^([\[51\]](#cite_note-zk9az-51))

[Solid-state hybrid drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid-state_hybrid_drive "Solid-state hybrid drive") (SSHDs) are based on the same principle, but integrate some amount of flash memory on board of a conventional drive instead of using a separate SSD. The flash layer in these drives can be accessed independently from the [magnetic storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_storage "Magnetic storage") by the host using [ATA-8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATA-8?action=edit&redlink=1 "ATA-8 (page does not exist)") commands, allowing the operating system to manage it. For example, Microsoft's [ReadyDrive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ReadyDrive "ReadyDrive") technology explicitly stores portions of the [hibernation file](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hibernation_(computing)#Microsoft_Windows "Hibernation (computing)") in the cache of these drives when the system hibernates, making the subsequent resume faster.^([\[52\]](#cite_note-RussinovichSolomon2009b-52))

[Dual-drive hybrid systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual-drive_hybrid_system "Dual-drive hybrid system") are combining the usage of separate SSD and HDD devices installed in the same computer, with overall performance optimization managed by the computer user, or by the computer's [operating system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operating_system "Operating system") software. Examples of this type of system are [bcache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bcache "Bcache") and [dm-cache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dm-cache "Dm-cache") on [Linux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linux "Linux"),^([\[53\]](#cite_note-q4ffn-53)) and Apple's [Fusion Drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fusion_Drive "Fusion Drive").

## Architecture and function

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Architecture and function")\]

The primary components of an SSD are the controller and the memory used to store data. Traditionally, early SSDs used volatile [DRAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DRAM "DRAM") for storage, but since 2009, most SSDs utilize non-volatile [NAND flash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAND_flash "NAND flash") memory, which retains data even when powered off.^([\[54\]](#cite_note-whatisssd-54)[\[3\]](#cite_note-SNIA-101-3)) Flash memory SSDs store data in [metal–oxide–semiconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOSFET "MOSFET") (MOS) integrated circuit chips, using non-volatile [floating-gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating-gate_MOSFET "Floating-gate MOSFET") memory cells.^([\[55\]](#cite_note-UsWco2-55))

### Controller

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Controller")\]

Main article: [Flash memory controller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_memory_controller "Flash memory controller")

Every SSD includes a controller, which manages the data flow between the NAND memory and the host computer. The controller is an embedded processor that runs firmware to optimize performance, managing data, and ensuring data integrity.^([\[56\]](#cite_note-Bechtolsheim2-56)[\[57\]](#cite_note-91nhJ2-57))

Some of the primary functions performed by the controller are:

- [Bad block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_sector "Bad sector") mapping
- [Read and write caching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cache_(computing) "Cache (computing)")
- [Encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encryption "Encryption")
- [Crypto-shredding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crypto-shredding "Crypto-shredding")
- [Error detection and correction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_detection_and_correction "Error detection and correction") using [error-correcting code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error-correcting_code "Error-correcting code") (ECC), such as [BCH code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BCH_code "BCH code")^([\[58\]](#cite_note-xbMNL2-58))
- [Garbage collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garbage_collection_(SSD)#Garbage_collection "Garbage collection (SSD)")
- [Read scrubbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_scrubbing "Memory scrubbing") and management of [read disturb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Read_disturb "Read disturb")
- [Wear leveling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wear_leveling "Wear leveling")
- Management of [flash memory refresh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_refresh "Memory refresh") and data retention^([\[59\]](#cite_note-59))
- Thermal detection and management

The overall performance of an SSD can scale with the number of parallel NAND chips and the efficiency of the controller. For example, controllers that enable parallel processing of NAND flash chips can improve bandwidth and reduce latency.^([\[60\]](#cite_note-SSD_Anthology-60)) The number of flash memory channel increases, the delay of raw flash memory (such as [ONFI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ONFI "ONFI") based [NAND flash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAND_flash "NAND flash")) decreases, and the bandwidth of raw flash memory increases.^([\[61\]](#cite_note-61))

Micron and Intel pioneered faster SSDs by implementing techniques such as data striping and interleaving to enhance read/write speeds.^([\[62\]](#cite_note-JSVZu2-62)) More recently, SandForce introduced controllers that incorporate data compression to reduce the amount of data written to the flash memory, potentially increasing both performance and endurance.^([\[63\]](#cite_note-Cgh8b2-63))

#### Wear leveling

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Wear leveling")\]

Main articles: [Wear leveling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wear_leveling "Wear leveling") and [Write amplification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Write_amplification "Write amplification")

Wear leveling is a technique used in SSDs to ensure that write and erase operations are distributed evenly across all blocks of the flash memory. Without this, specific blocks could wear out prematurely due to repeated use, reducing the overall lifespan of the SSD. The process moves data that is infrequently changed (cold data) from heavily used blocks, so that data that changes more frequently (hot data) can be written to those blocks. This helps distribute wear more evenly across the entire SSD. However, this process introduces additional writes, known as write amplification, which must be managed to balance performance and durability.^([\[64\]](#cite_note-nHbfg2-64)[\[65\]](#cite_note-LNyYx2-65))

### Memory

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Memory")\]

#### Flash memory

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Flash memory")\]

**Comparison of architectures \[ 66 \]**

| Comparison characteristics | [MLC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-level_cell) : [SLC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-level_cell) | [NAND](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAND_Flash) : [NOR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NOR_flash) |
|----------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Persistence ratio          | 1 : 10                                                                                                         | 1 : 10                                                                                            |
| Sequential write ratio     | 1 : 3                                                                                                          | 1 : 4                                                                                             |
| Sequential read ratio      | 1 : 1                                                                                                          | 1 : 5                                                                                             |
| Price ratio                | 1 : 1.3                                                                                                        |                                                                                                   |

Most SSDs use non-volatile [NAND](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAND_gate "NAND gate") flash memory for data storage, primarily due to its cost-effectiveness and ability to retain data without a constant power supply. NAND flash-based SSDs store data in semiconductor cells, with the specific architecture influencing performance, endurance, and cost.^([\[67\]](#cite_note-tv599-67))

There are various types of NAND flash memory, categorized by the number of bits stored in each cell:

- Single-Level Cell (SLC): Stores 1 bit per cell. SLC provides the highest performance, reliability, and endurance but is more expensive.
- Multi-Level Cell (MLC): Stores 2 bits per cell. MLC offers a balance between cost, performance, and endurance.
- Triple-Level Cell (TLC): Stores 3 bits per cell. TLC is less expensive but slower and less durable than SLC and MLC.
- Quad-Level Cell (QLC): Stores 4 bits per cell. QLC is the most affordable option but has the lowest performance and endurance.^([\[68\]](#cite_note-CWorldLackluster3-68))

Over time, SSD controllers have improved the efficiency of NAND flash, incorporating techniques such as [interleaved memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interleaved_memory "Interleaved memory"), advanced error correction, and wear leveling to optimize performance and extend the lifespan of the drive.^([\[69\]](#cite_note-FGplb-69)[\[70\]](#cite_note-Drossel-70)[\[71\]](#cite_note-mlc-slc2-71)[\[72\]](#cite_note-Lucchesi-72)[\[73\]](#cite_note-Jim_Bagley-73)) Lower-end SSDs often use QLC or TLC memory, while higher-end drives for enterprise or performance-critical applications may use MLC or SLC.^([\[71\]](#cite_note-mlc-slc2-71))

In addition to the flat (planar) NAND structure, many SSDs now use 3D NAND (or V-NAND), where memory cells are stacked vertically, increasing storage density while improving performance and reducing costs.^([\[74\]](#cite_note-VOyVx2-74))

#### DRAM and DIMM

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: DRAM and DIMM")\]

Some SSDs use volatile DRAM instead of NAND flash, offering very high-speed data access but requiring a constant power supply to retain data. DRAM-based SSDs are typically used in specialized applications where performance is prioritized over cost or non-volatility. Many SSDs, such as [NVDIMM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NVDIMM "NVDIMM") devices, are equipped with backup power sources such as internal batteries or external AC/DC adapters. These power sources ensure data is transferred to a backup system (usually NAND flash or another storage medium) in the event of power loss, preventing data corruption or loss.^([\[75\]](#cite_note-BiTMICRO2-75)[\[76\]](#cite_note-BgFad2-76)) Similarly, [ULLtraDIMM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ULLtraDIMM "ULLtraDIMM") devices use components designed for DIMM modules, but only use flash memory, similar to a DRAM SSD.^([\[77\]](#cite_note-4xj0F-77))

DRAM-based SSDs are often used for tasks where data must be accessed at high speeds with low latency, such as in high-performance computing or certain server environments.^([\[78\]](#cite_note-6VNR12-78))

#### 3D XPoint

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: 3D XPoint")\]

3D XPoint is a type of non-volatile memory technology developed by Intel and Micron, announced in 2015.^([\[79\]](#cite_note-extremetech2-79)) It operates by changing the electrical resistance of materials in its cells, offering much faster access times than NAND flash. 3D XPoint-based SSDs, such as Intel's Optane drives, provide lower latency and higher endurance than NAND-based drives, although they are more expensive per gigabyte.^([\[80\]](#cite_note-anandtechJuly20152-80)[\[81\]](#cite_note-cnet20152-81))

#### Other

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Other")\]

Drives known as [hybrid drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybrid_drive "Hybrid drive") or [solid-state hybrid drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid-state_hybrid_drive "Solid-state hybrid drive") (SSHDs) use a hybrid of spinning disks and flash memory.^([\[82\]](#cite_note-vQDKb-82)[\[83\]](#cite_note-ouSAC-83)) Some SSDs use [magnetoresistive random-access memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetoresistive_random-access_memory "Magnetoresistive random-access memory") (MRAM) for storing data.^([\[84\]](#cite_note-0cwDb-84)[\[85\]](#cite_note-8cbyg-85))

### Cache and buffer

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Cache and buffer")\]

Many flash-based SSDs include a small amount of volatile DRAM as a cache, similar to the [buffers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_buffer "Disk buffer") in hard disk drives. This cache can temporarily hold data while it is being written to the flash memory, and it also stores metadata such as the mapping of logical blocks to physical locations on the SSD.^([\[60\]](#cite_note-SSD_Anthology-60)) This cache may also temporarily hold data while it being recently read from the flash memory.

Some SSD controllers, like those from SandForce, achieve high performance without using an external DRAM cache. These designs rely on other mechanisms, such as on-chip SRAM, to manage data and minimize power consumption.^([\[86\]](#cite_note-Demerjian_TPC-C_Records2-86))

Additionally, some SSDs use an [SLC buffer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Write_amplification "Write amplification") mechanism to temporarily store data in single-level cell (SLC) mode, even on multi-level cell (MLC) or triple-level cell (TLC) SSDs. This improves write performance by allowing data to be written to faster SLC storage before being moved to slower, higher-capacity MLC or TLC storage.^([\[87\]](#cite_note-GEsrf2-87))

On NVMe SSDs, Host Memory Buffer (HMB) technology allows the SSD to use a portion of the system's DRAM instead of relying on a built-in DRAM cache, reducing costs while maintaining a high level of read performance.^([\[86\]](#cite_note-Demerjian_TPC-C_Records2-86)) In practice, on consumer SSDs with HMB, the HMB is mainly used for cache the file table mappings, because HMB lacks power loss protection (PLP) mechanism.

In value consumer SSDs, DRAM are mainly used for cache the file table mappings. In certain high-end consumer and enterprise SSDs, larger amounts of DRAM are included to cache the file table mappings and buffer the written data, reducing write amplification and enhances overall performance.^([\[88\]](#cite_note-K6T913-88))

### Battery and supercapacitor

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Battery and supercapacitor")\]

Higher-performing SSDs may include capacitors, supercapacitors or batteries, which helps preserve data integrity in the event of an unexpected power loss. The capacitor or battery provides enough power to allow the data in the cache to be written to the non-volatile memory, ensuring no data is lost.^([\[86\]](#cite_note-Demerjian_TPC-C_Records2-86)[\[89\]](#cite_note-hHUsG2-89))

In some SSDs that use multi-level cell (MLC) flash memory, a potential issue known as "lower page corruption" can occur if power is lost while programming an upper page. This can result in previously written data becoming corrupted. To address this, some high-end SSDs incorporate [supercapacitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercapacitor "Supercapacitor") to ensure all data can be safely written during a sudden power loss.^([\[90\]](#cite_note-Werner_SSD_Features-90))

Some consumer SSDs have built-in capacitors to save critical data such as the [Flash Translation Layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_memory_controller "Flash memory controller") (FTL) mapping table. Examples include the Crucial M500 and Intel 320 series.^([\[88\]](#cite_note-K6T913-88)) Enterprise-class SSDs, such as the Intel DC S3700 series, often come with more robust power-loss protection mechanisms like supercapacitors or batteries.^([\[91\]](#cite_note-bdQ9x2-91))

### Host Interface

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section: Host Interface")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e4/M.2_2242_SSD_connected_into_USB_3.0_adapter.jpg/250px-M.2_2242_SSD_connected_into_USB_3.0_adapter.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:M.2_2242_SSD_connected_into_USB_3.0_adapter.jpg)

An [M.2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M.2 "M.2") SATA (2242) solid-state-drive (SSD) connected into [USB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USB "USB") 3.0 adapter and connected to computer

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a1/Mushkin_Ventura_USB-SSD.jpg/250px-Mushkin_Ventura_USB-SSD.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Mushkin_Ventura_USB-SSD.jpg)

Mushkin Ventura, A USB that has an SSD inside

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0c/Viper_PVP30_Portable_SSD_2_TB-3001.jpg/250px-Viper_PVP30_Portable_SSD_2_TB-3001.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Viper_PVP30_Portable_SSD_2_TB-3001.jpg)

Portable SSD with connectors [USB-A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USB_hardware#Connectors "USB hardware") (front) and [USB-C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USB_hardware#Connectors "USB hardware") (back), specification [3.2 Gen 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USB#Signaling "USB") with [data transfer rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USB#Signaling "USB") 10 Gbit/s, capacity 2 [Terabyte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byte#Units_based_on_powers_of_10 "Byte")

The host interface of an SSD refers to the physical connector and the signaling methods used to communicate between the SSD and the host system. This interface is managed by the SSD's controller and is often similar to those found in traditional hard disk drives (HDDs). Common interfaces include:

- [Serial ATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_ATA "Serial ATA"): One of the most widely used interfaces in consumer SSDs. SATA 3.0 supports transfer speeds up to 6.0 Gbit/s.^([\[92\]](#cite_note-Ad1qY2-92))
- [Serial attached SCSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_attached_SCSI "Serial attached SCSI"): Primarily used in enterprise environments, SAS interfaces are faster and more robust than SATA. SAS 3.0 offers speeds of up to 12.0 Gbit/s.^([\[93\]](#cite_note-roadmap2-93))
- [PCI Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PCI_Express "PCI Express") (PCIe): A high-speed interface used in high-performance SSDs. PCIe 3.0 x4 supports transfer speeds of up to 31.5 Gbit/s.^([\[94\]](#cite_note-faq32-94))
- [M.2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M.2 "M.2"): A newer interface designed for SSDs that is more compact than SATA or PCIe, often found in laptops and desktops. M.2 supports both SATA (up to 6.0 Gbit/s) and PCIe interfaces.
- [U.2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.2 "U.2"): Another interface used for enterprise-grade SSDs, providing PCIe x4 lanes but with a more robust connector suitable for server environments.
- [Fibre Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibre_Channel "Fibre Channel"): Typically used in enterprise systems, Fibre Channel interfaces offer high data transfer speeds, with modern versions supporting up to 128 Gbit/s.
- [USB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USB "USB"): Many external SSDs use the Universal Serial Bus interface, with modern versions like [USB 3.1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USB_3.1 "USB 3.1") Gen 2 supporting speeds of up to 10 Gbit/s.^([\[95\]](#cite_note-HPT692-95))
- [Thunderbolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thunderbolt_(interface) "Thunderbolt (interface)"): Some high-end external SSDs use the Thunderbolt interface.
- [Parallel ATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_ATA "Parallel ATA") (PATA): An older interface used in early SSDs, with speeds up to 1064 Mbit/s. PATA has largely been replaced by [SATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SATA "SATA") due to higher data transfer rates and greater reliability.^([\[96\]](#cite_note-RKxU12-96)[\[97\]](#cite_note-4uTmC2-97))
- Parallel [SCSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SCSI "SCSI"): An interface primarily used in servers, with speeds ranging from 40 Mbit/s to 2560 Mbit/s. It has mostly been replaced by Serial Attached SCSI. The last [SCSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SCSI "SCSI")-based SSD was introduced in 2004.^([\[98\]](#cite_note-WubOc2-98))

SSDs may support various logical interfaces, which define the command sets used by operating systems to communicate with the SSD. Two common logical interfaces include:

- [Advanced Host Controller Interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Host_Controller_Interface "Advanced Host Controller Interface") (AHCI): Initially designed for HDDs, AHCI is commonly used with SATA SSDs but is less efficient for modern SSDs due to its overhead.
- [NVM Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NVM_Express "NVM Express") (NVMe): A modern interface designed specifically for SSDs, NVMe takes full advantage of the parallelism in SSDs, providing significantly lower latency and higher throughput than AHCI.^([\[3\]](#cite_note-SNIA-101-3))

## Configurations

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Configurations")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1b/Intel_525_mSATA_SSD.jpg/250px-Intel_525_mSATA_SSD.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Intel_525_mSATA_SSD.jpg)

Intel 525 mSATA SSD

The size and shape of any device are largely driven by the size and shape of the components used to make that device. Traditional HDDs and [optical drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_disc_drive "Optical disc drive") are designed around the rotating [platter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_drive_platter "Hard disk drive platter")(s) or [optical disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_disc "Optical disc") along with the [spindle motor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_drive_spindle "Hard disk drive spindle") inside. Since an SSD is made up of various interconnected [integrated circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_circuit "Integrated circuit") (ICs) and an interface connector, its shape is no longer limited to the shape of rotating media drives. The lack of moving parts and light weight meant that an SSD can have no shell and simply appear in the shape of a plug-in board. On the other end of the size spectrum, some solid-state storage solutions come in a larger chassis that may even be a rack-mount form factor with numerous SSDs inside. They would all connect to a common bus inside the chassis and connect outside the box with a single connector.^([\[3\]](#cite_note-SNIA-101-3))

For general computer use, the 2.5-inch form factor (typically found in laptops and used for most SATA SSDs) was the most popular in the 2010s, in three thicknesses^([\[99\]](#cite_note-99)) (7.0mm, 9.5mm, 14.8 or 15.0mm; with 12.0mm also available for some models). For desktop computers with 3.5-inch hard disk drive slots, a simple adapter plate can be used to make such a drive fit. Other types of form factors are more common in enterprise applications. An SSD can also be completely integrated in the other circuitry of the device, as in the [Apple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Inc. "Apple Inc.") [MacBook Air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacBook_Air "MacBook Air") (starting with the fall 2010 model).^([\[100\]](#cite_note-RXXdi-100)) As of 2014^([\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit)), [mSATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MSATA "MSATA") and [M.2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M.2 "M.2") form factors also gained popularity, primarily in laptops.

### Standard HDD form factors

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: Standard HDD form factors")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f0/Samsung_SSD_840_120GB_MZ-7TD120--4_LID_REMOVED.JPG/250px-Samsung_SSD_840_120GB_MZ-7TD120--4_LID_REMOVED.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Samsung_SSD_840_120GB_MZ-7TD120--4_LID_REMOVED.JPG)

An SSD with a 2.5-inch HDD form factor. The SSD is opened to show what is inside. It contains the controller, DRAM memory, and four NAND flash. Each NAND is 32GB.

The benefit of using a current [HDD form factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HDD_form_factor "HDD form factor") would be to take advantage of the extensive infrastructure already in place to mount and connect the drives to the host system.^([\[3\]](#cite_note-SNIA-101-3)[\[101\]](#cite_note-aGGBC-101)) These traditional form factors are known by the size of the rotating media (i.e., 5.25-inch, 3.5-inch, 2.5-inch or 1.8-inch) and not the dimensions of the drive casing.

#### Disk-on-a-module form factors

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section: Disk-on-a-module form factors")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2e/Disk_on_module.jpg/250px-Disk_on_module.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Disk_on_module.jpg)

A 2 GB disk-on-a-module with PATA interface

A *disk-on-a-module* (*DOM*) is a flash drive with either 40/44-pin [Parallel ATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_ATA "Parallel ATA") (PATA) or [SATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SATA "SATA") interface, intended to be plugged directly into the motherboard and used as a computer [hard disk drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_drive "Hard disk drive") (HDD). DOM devices emulate a traditional hard disk drive, resulting in no need for special drivers or other specific operating system support. DOMs are usually used in [embedded systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embedded_system "Embedded system"), which are often deployed in harsh environments where mechanical HDDs would simply fail, or in [thin clients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thin_client "Thin client") because of small size, low power consumption, and silent operation.

SATA DOMs come in several forms. The most traditional emulates the standard interface, with the 7-pin SATA data connector placed beside the 15-pin SATA power connector. An example is the "half-slim SATA" MO-297 size standard. To save board space, smaller SATA DOMs were made that only use the SATA data connector. The earliest type, made by e.g. [Supermicro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supermicro "Supermicro"), relied on a separate [Berg connector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berg_connector "Berg connector") to deliver power. A second type of 2012, made by [Innodisk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Innodisk "Innodisk"), repurposes the 7th pin of the connector from GND to VCC (+5V).^([\[102\]](#cite_note-u6dAh-102)) A third type called "pin 8 power" replaces the two plastic structural elements on the sides with two metal contacts for GND and VCC.^([\[103\]](#cite_note-103)) These new types of SATADOMs are now so popular that the older 7+15-pin type is virtually not considered to be "SATADOMs" any more, especially as few motherboards provide such an interface. (PATA DOMs have no power concern as the connector supplies 3.3V or 5V power: the same way a [CompactFlash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CompactFlash "CompactFlash") would get its power.)

There are also USB DOMs designed to be plugging into the USB 2.0 header pins on a motherboard.

As of 2016,^([\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit)) DOM storage capacities range from 4 MB to 128 GB with different variations in physical layouts, including vertical or horizontal orientation.^(\[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*\])

### Standard small card form factors

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=23 "Edit section: Standard small card form factors")\]

Main articles: [mSATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MSATA "MSATA") and [M.2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M.2 "M.2")

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5f/128GB_2230_NVME_SSD.jpg/250px-128GB_2230_NVME_SSD.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:128GB_2230_NVME_SSD.jpg)

As this NVMe SSD is only 2230 size, it has a smaller controller chip with only one NAND flash without any DRAM memory.

For applications where space is at a premium, like for ultrabooks or [tablet computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tablet_computer "Tablet computer"), a few compact form factors were standardized for flash-based SSDs.

There is the mSATA form factor, which uses the [PCI Express Mini Card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PCI_Express_Mini_Card "PCI Express Mini Card") physical layout. It remains electrically compatible with the PCI Express Mini Card interface specification while requiring an additional connection to the SATA host controller through the same connector. A higher-performance SSD may use the Mini-PCIe to access the PCIe bus directly.

[M.2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M.2 "M.2") form factor, formerly known as the Next Generation Form Factor (NGFF), is a natural transition from the mSATA and physical layout it used, to a more usable and more advanced form factor. While mSATA took advantage of an existing form factor and connector, M.2 has been designed to maximize usage of the card space, while minimizing the footprint. The M.2 standard allows both SATA and [PCI Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PCI_Express "PCI Express") SSDs to be fitted onto M.2 modules.^([\[104\]](#cite_note-n8OOK-104))

### Add-in-card form factors

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=24 "Edit section: Add-in-card form factors")\]

Before M.2 was standardized, one of the main ways to access the PCIe bus for faster-than-SATA/SAS speed on a server was through the PCIe slot. A common shape is called HHHL (Half Height Half Length), or AIC (Add in Card) SSDs.^([\[105\]](#cite_note-105)[\[106\]](#cite_note-106)[\[107\]](#cite_note-107))

Some primitive PCIe SSDs do not access the PCIe bus directly, but simply had a PCIe-to-SATA/SAS bridge device and a number of SATA or SAS [flash controllers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_controller "Flash controller") attached. This was considered acceptable in 2010,^([\[108\]](#cite_note-EQzBc-108)) when true PCIe SSDs were still new.^([\[109\]](#cite_note-KUBX0-109))

This shape remains in use for some high performance, high capacity drives. The PCIe slot offers 16 lanes of data and 75 watts of power, still much larger than what a M.2 slot can provide. It also provides space for a large [heat sink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_sink "Heat sink"). There are also adapter boards that converts other form factors, especially M.2 drives with PCIe interface, into regular add-in cards.

### Ball grid array form factors

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=25 "Edit section: Ball grid array form factors")\]

In the early 2000s, a few companies introduced SSDs in [Ball Grid Array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball_Grid_Array "Ball Grid Array") (BGA) form factors, such as M-Systems' (now [SanDisk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SanDisk "SanDisk")) DiskOnChip^([\[110\]](#cite_note-afmCP-110)) and [Silicon Storage Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_Storage_Technology "Silicon Storage Technology")'s NANDrive^([\[111\]](#cite_note-qP23g-111)[\[112\]](#cite_note-qcnzi-112)) (now produced by [Greenliant Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenliant_Systems "Greenliant Systems")), and Memoright's M1000^([\[113\]](#cite_note-W4Bm8-113)) for use in embedded systems. The main benefits of BGA SSDs are their low power consumption, small chip package size to fit into compact subsystems, and that they can be [soldered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soldered "Soldered") directly onto a system motherboard to reduce adverse effects from vibration and shock.^([\[114\]](#cite_note-duhuh-114))

Such embedded drives now often adhere to the [eMMC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EMMC "EMMC") and [eUFS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Flash_Storage "Universal Flash Storage") standards.

### Form factors with nonstandard connectors

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=26 "Edit section: Form factors with nonstandard connectors")\]

#### Box

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=27 "Edit section: Box")\]

Many of the DRAM-based solutions in 2014 use a box that is often designed to fit in a rack-mount system. The number of [DRAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_random-access_memory "Dynamic random-access memory") components required to get sufficient capacity to store the data along with the backup power supplies requires a larger space than traditional HDD form factors.^([\[115\]](#cite_note-UKh9v-115))

#### Board/card

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=28 "Edit section: Board/card")\]

The flexibility of the SSD also allows for many unusual form factors, some of which had been important in its early adoption in PCs.^([\[116\]](#cite_note-aXpRJ-116)) For example, the SATADIMM from Viking Technology uses an empty DDR3 DIMM slot on the motherboard to provide power to the SSD with a separate SATA connector to provide the data connection back to the computer. The result is an easy-to-install SSD with a capacity equal to drives that typically take a full 2.5-inch [drive bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drive_bay "Drive bay").^([\[117\]](#cite_note-qIFdQ-117))

## Development and history

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=29 "Edit section: Development and history")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8f/Historical_cost_of_computer_memory_and_storage.svg/250px-Historical_cost_of_computer_memory_and_storage.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Historical_cost_of_computer_memory_and_storage.svg)

Historical lowest retail prices of [computer memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_memory "Computer memory") and [storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_storage "Computer storage")

### Early SSDs using RAM and similar technology

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=30 "Edit section: Early SSDs using RAM and similar technology")\]

The first devices resembling solid-state drives (SSDs) used semiconductor technology, with an early example being the [1978 StorageTek STC 4305](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1978_StorageTek_STC_4305?action=edit&redlink=1 "1978 StorageTek STC 4305 (page does not exist)"). This device was a plug-compatible replacement for the [IBM 2305](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_2305 "IBM 2305") hard drive, initially using [charge-coupled devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge-coupled_devices "Charge-coupled devices") for storage and later switching to [dynamic random-access memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_random-access_memory "Dynamic random-access memory") (DRAM). The STC 4305 was significantly faster than its mechanical counterparts and cost around \$400,000 for a 45 MB capacity.^([\[118\]](#cite_note-QqWhy-118)) Though early SSD-like devices existed, they were not widely used due to their high cost and small storage capacity.

In the late 1980s, companies like Zitel began selling DRAM-based SSD products under the name "RAMDisk." These devices were primarily used in specialized systems like those made by UNIVAC and Perkin-Elmer.

Top and bottom sides of a 100GB Intel DC S3700 SATA SSD and a 120GB Intel 535 mSATA SSD.

Flash memory, a key component in modern SSDs, was invented in 1980 by [Fujio Masuoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fujio_Masuoka "Fujio Masuoka") at Toshiba.^([\[127\]](#cite_note-LR4Sv-127)[\[128\]](#cite_note-PsRxx-128)) Flash-based SSDs were patented in 1989 by the founders of [SanDisk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SanDisk "SanDisk"),^([\[129\]](#cite_note-uZUYs-129)) which released its first product in 1991: a 20 MB SSD for IBM laptops.^([\[130\]](#cite_note-mOVpl-130)) While the storage capacity was limited and the price high (around \$1,000), this marked the beginning of a transition to flash memory as an alternative to traditional hard drives.^([\[131\]](#cite_note-computer_history-131))

In the 1990s, new manufacturers of flash memory drives emerged, including [STEC, Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STEC,_Inc. "STEC, Inc."),^([\[132\]](#cite_note-xScJY-132)) [M-Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M-Systems "M-Systems"),^([\[133\]](#cite_note-Odagiri-133)[\[134\]](#cite_note-ilGjH-134)) and [BiTMICRO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BiTMICRO?action=edit&redlink=1 "BiTMICRO (page does not exist)").^([\[135\]](#cite_note-kiTyE-135)[\[136\]](#cite_note-OnLi9-136))

As the technology advanced, SSDs saw dramatic improvements in capacity, speed, and affordability.^([\[137\]](#cite_note-DCoih-137)[\[138\]](#cite_note-2Xelp-138)[\[139\]](#cite_note-NnTqi-139)[\[140\]](#cite_note-Daily_Tech_SSD-140)) By 2016, commercially available SSDs had more capacity than the largest available HDDs.^([\[141\]](#cite_note-Nsx29-141)[\[142\]](#cite_note-JfPBO-142)[\[143\]](#cite_note-HKt3E-143)[\[144\]](#cite_note-A7jmh-144)[\[145\]](#cite_note-ZSdfU-145)) By 2018, flash-based SSDs had reached capacities of up to 100 TB in enterprise products, with consumer SSDs offering up to 16 TB.^([\[119\]](#cite_note-Jyarq-119)) These advancements were accompanied by significant increases in read and write speeds, with some high-end consumer models reaching speeds of up to 14.5 GB/s.^([\[121\]](#cite_note-OkyThn-121))

In 2021, NVMe 2.0 with Zoned Namespaces (ZNS) was announced. ZNS allows data to be mapped directly to its physical location in memory, providing direct access on an SSD without a flash translation layer.^([\[146\]](#cite_note-146)) In 2024, Samsung announced what it called the world's first SSD with a hybrid PCIe interface, the Samsung 990 EVO. The hybrid interface runs in either the x4 PCIe 4.0 or x2 PCIe 5.0 modes, a first for an M.2 SSD.^([\[147\]](#cite_note-147))

SSD prices have also fallen dramatically, with the cost per gigabyte decreasing from around \$50,000 in 1991 to less than \$0.05 by 2020.^([\[126\]](#cite_note-u7scU-126))

### Enterprise flash drives

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=32 "Edit section: Enterprise flash drives")\]

Enterprise flash drives (EFDs) are designed for high-performance applications requiring fast input/output operations per second ([IOPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IOPS "IOPS")), reliability, and energy efficiency. EFDs often have higher specifications than consumer SSDs, making them suitable for mission-critical applications. The term was first used by EMC in 2008 to describe SSDs built for enterprise environments.^([\[148\]](#cite_note-HjAAB-148)[\[149\]](#cite_note-eA2wW-149))

One example of an EFD is the Intel DC S3700 series, launched in 2012. These drives were notable for their consistent performance, maintaining IOPS variation within a narrow range, which is crucial for enterprise environments.^([\[150\]](#cite_note-tsmg9-150))

Another significant product is the Toshiba PX02SS series, launched in 2016. Designed for write-intensive applications like online transaction processing, these drives achieved impressive read and write speeds and high endurance ratings.^([\[151\]](#cite_note-sPOpW-151))

### Drives using other persistent memory technologies

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=33 "Edit section: Drives using other persistent memory technologies")\]

In 2017, Intel introduced SSDs based on 3D XPoint technology under the Optane brand. Unlike NAND flash, 3D XPoint uses a different method to store data, offering higher IOPS performance, although sequential read and write speeds remain slower compared to traditional SSDs.^([\[152\]](#cite_note-xxSiK-152))

### Consumer use

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=34 "Edit section: Consumer use")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d5/MacBook_Air_Mid_2012.png/250px-MacBook_Air_Mid_2012.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:MacBook_Air_Mid_2012.png)

The [MacBook Air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacBook_Air "MacBook Air") and [Ultrabooks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrabooks "Ultrabooks") are the earliest popular implementations of SSD. Alongside inarguable faster speed resulting in absolutely-better systems' performance, SSD are also thinner and smaller than HDD, allowing modern laptops to be lighter and sleeker without memory-related compromise of productivity.

As SSD technology continues to improve, they are increasingly used in ultra-mobile PCs and lightweight laptop systems. The first flash-memory SSD based PC to become available was the Sony Vaio UX90, announced for pre-order on 27 June 2006 and began shipping in Japan on 3 July 2006 with a 16 GB flash memory hard drive.^([\[153\]](#cite_note-6IWdz-153)) Another of the first mainstream releases of SSD was the [XO Laptop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OLPC_XO-1 "OLPC XO-1"), built as part of the [One Laptop Per Child](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Laptop_Per_Child "One Laptop Per Child") project. Mass production of these computers, built for children in developing countries, began in December 2007. By 2009, [Dell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dell "Dell"),^([\[154\]](#cite_note-dellflash-154)[\[155\]](#cite_note-LmHYr-155)[\[156\]](#cite_note-pRmfx-156)) [Toshiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toshiba "Toshiba"),^([\[157\]](#cite_note-AxQd3-157)[\[158\]](#cite_note-ywjLY-158)) [Asus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asus "Asus"),^([\[159\]](#cite_note-G10Dt-159)) [Apple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Inc. "Apple Inc."),^([\[160\]](#cite_note-macbookairspec-160)) and [Lenovo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenovo "Lenovo")^([\[161\]](#cite_note-lJ9mp-161)) had begun producing laptops with SSDs.

By 2010, Apple's [MacBook Air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacBook_Air "MacBook Air") line began using solid state drives as the default.^([\[162\]](#cite_note-sjc2m-162)[\[160\]](#cite_note-macbookairspec-160)) In 2011, Intel's [Ultrabook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrabook "Ultrabook") became the first widely available consumer computers using SSDs aside from the MacBook Air.^([\[163\]](#cite_note-X97JR-163)) At present, SSD devices are widely used and distributed by a [number of companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_computer_hardware_manufacturers#Solid-state_drives_(SSDs) "List of computer hardware manufacturers"), with a small number of companies manufacturing the NAND flash devices within them.^([\[164\]](#cite_note-GPLS6-164))

### Sales

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=35 "Edit section: Sales")\]

SSD shipments were approximately 11 million units in 2009,^([\[165\]](#cite_note-Sw0vx-165)) rising to 17.3 million units in 2011^([\[166\]](#cite_note-i2013-04-05-166)) for a total market value of US\$5 billion.^([\[167\]](#cite_note-WO6FY-167)) Shipments continued to grow to 39 million units in 2012 and were projected to reach 83 million units in 2013,^([\[168\]](#cite_note-E4yw8-168)) 201.4 million units in 2016,^([\[166\]](#cite_note-i2013-04-05-166)) and 227 million units in 2017.^([\[169\]](#cite_note-tg2013-05-09-169))

*Tom's Hardware*, citing a 2024 analysis from Yole Group, projected that SSD revenues will rise from USD 29 billion in 2022 to USD 67 billion by 2028.^([\[170\]](#cite_note-170))

The global solid-state drive (SSD) market is projected to grow significantly between 2024 and 2030, driven by rising demand for data center expansion, cloud computing services, and consumer electronics upgrades.^([\[171\]](#cite_note-GVR-171)) In a 2024 report, Grand View Research estimated the SSD market at USD 19.1 billion in 2023 and projected it to reach USD 55.1 billion by 2030.^([\[171\]](#cite_note-GVR-171)) In a separate 2024 study, Mordor Intelligence valued the market at USD 63.45 billion for 2024, forecasting growth to USD 172.82 billion by 2030.^([\[172\]](#cite_note-172))

## File-system support

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=36 "Edit section: File-system support")\]

Main article: [File systems optimized for flash memory, solid state media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_file_systems#File_systems_optimized_for_flash_memory,_solid_state_media "List of file systems")

The same [file systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File_system "File system") used on hard disk drives can typically also be used on solid state drives. File systems that support SSDs generally also support the TRIM command, which helps the SSD to recycle discarded data. The file system does not need to manage [wear leveling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wear_leveling "Wear leveling") or other flash memory characteristics, as they are handled internally by the SSD. Some [log-structured file systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Log-structured_file_system "Log-structured file system") (e.g. [F2FS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F2FS "F2FS"), [JFFS2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JFFS2 "JFFS2")) help to reduce [write amplification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Write_amplification "Write amplification") on SSDs, especially in situations where only very small amounts of data are changed, such as when updating [file-system metadata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File_system#Metadata "File system").

If an operating system does not support using TRIM on discrete [swap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paging "Paging") partitions, it might be possible to use swap files inside an ordinary file system instead. For example, macOS does not support swap partitions; it only swaps to files within a file system, so it can use TRIM when, for example, swap files are deleted.^(\[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*\])

### Linux

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=37 "Edit section: Linux")\]

Since 2010, standard Linux drive utilities have taken care of appropriate partition alignment by default.^([\[173\]](#cite_note-hovkL-173))

Kernel support for the TRIM operation was introduced in version 2.6.33 of the Linux kernel mainline, released on 24 February 2010.^([\[174\]](#cite_note-Om4z2-174)) The [ext4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ext4 "Ext4"), [Btrfs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Btrfs "Btrfs"), [XFS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XFS "XFS"), [JFS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JFS_(file_system) "JFS (file system)"), and [F2FS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F2FS "F2FS") file systems include support for the discard (TRIM or UNMAP) function. Non-native file systems such as [exFAT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ExFAT "ExFAT") and [NTFS-3G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NTFS-3G "NTFS-3G") also support TRIM. To automatically make use of TRIM on file deletion, a file system must be mounted using the `discard` parameter. Linux [swap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paging "Paging") partitions are by default performing discard operations when the underlying drive supports TRIM, with the possibility to turn them off.^([\[175\]](#cite_note-man-8-swapon-175)[\[176\]](#cite_note-debian-ssd-176)[\[177\]](#cite_note-3.12.5-swapfile.c-177)) Support for queued TRIM, a [SATA 3.1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SATA_3.1 "SATA 3.1") feature that results in TRIM commands not disrupting the command queues, was introduced in Linux kernel 3.12, released on November 2, 2013.^([\[178\]](#cite_note-u6N4X-178))

An alternative to the kernel-level TRIM operation is to use a user-space utility called `fstrim` that goes through all of the unused blocks in a filesystem and dispatches TRIM commands for those areas. The`fstrim`utility is usually run by [cron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cron "Cron") or [systemd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systemd "Systemd") as a scheduled task. A filesystem that supports TRIM remains required.^([\[179\]](#cite_note-pmNYx-179))

Whether formatting or resizing trims the unused space depends on the implementation. For example, the `mke2fs` program for formatting ext2/3/4 defaults to issuing a TRIM command (if supported) to the entire block,^([\[180\]](#cite_note-180)) but the `resize2fs` program for resizing ext2/3/4 does not TRIM the space left unused after shrinking.^([\[181\]](#cite_note-181)) TRIM-after-resize is instead done by `fdisk` or `sfdisk`, programs that edit the partition table.^([\[182\]](#cite_note-182))

In addition, [bcache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bcache "Bcache") is designed to have an SSD act as a read/write cache for a slower drive such as an HDD.^([\[183\]](#cite_note-183))

### macOS

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=38 "Edit section: macOS")\]

Versions since Mac OS X 10.6.8 (Snow Leopard) support TRIM but only when used with an Apple-purchased SSD.^([\[184\]](#cite_note-3JN4a-184)) TRIM is not automatically enabled for third-party drives, except for external removable SSDs, although it can be enabled by using third-party utilities such as *Trim Enabler*. The status of TRIM can be checked in the System Information application or in the `system_profiler` command-line tool.

Versions since OS X 10.10.4 (Yosemite) include `sudo trimforce enable` as a Terminal command that enables TRIM on non-Apple SSDs.^([\[185\]](#cite_note-tGORd-185)) There is also a technique to enable TRIM in versions earlier than Mac OS X 10.6.8, although it remains uncertain whether TRIM is actually utilized properly in those cases.^([\[186\]](#cite_note-07z3l-186))

### Microsoft Windows

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=39 "Edit section: Microsoft Windows")\]

Prior to Windows 7, Microsoft Windows did not take any specific measures to support solid state drives. From Windows 7, the standard [NTFS file system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NTFS "NTFS") provides support for the TRIM command.^([\[187\]](#cite_note-xoQ7F-187))

By default, Windows 7 and newer versions execute TRIM commands automatically if the device is detected to be a solid-state drive *and* the filesystem driver supports TRIM (NTFS or ReFS). However, because TRIM irreversibly resets all freed space, it may be desirable to disable support where enabling data recovery is preferred over wear leveling.^([\[188\]](#cite_note-cKwlG-188)) Windows implements TRIM for more than just file-delete operations. The TRIM operation is integrated with partition- and volume-level commands such as *format* and *delete*, with file-system commands relating to truncate and compression, and with the System Restore (also known as Volume Snapshot) feature.^([\[189\]](#cite_note-:1-189))

[Defragmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defragmentation "Defragmentation") should be disabled on solid-state drives because the location of the file components on an SSD does not significantly impact its performance, but moving the files to make them [contiguous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contiguous_data_storage "Contiguous data storage") using the [Windows Defrag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_Defrag "Windows Defrag") routine will cause unnecessary write wear on the limited number of write cycles on the SSD. The [SuperFetch feature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SuperFetch_feature "SuperFetch feature") will also not materially improve performance and causes additional overhead in the system and SSD.^([\[190\]](#cite_note-w8zKb-190)) Since Windows 8.1, the [Windows Defrag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_Defrag "Windows Defrag") routine would instead "retrim" (TRIM) partitions detected as SSDs.^([\[191\]](#cite_note-defrag8-191))

#### Windows Vista

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=40 "Edit section: Windows Vista")\]

[Windows Vista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_Vista "Windows Vista") generally expects hard disk drives rather than SSDs.^([\[192\]](#cite_note-D2PjZ-192)[\[193\]](#cite_note-CWorldSpeedupSSD-193)) [Windows Vista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_Vista "Windows Vista") includes [ReadyBoost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ReadyBoost "ReadyBoost") to exploit characteristics of USB-connected flash devices, but for SSDs it only improves the default partition alignment to prevent read-modify-write operations that reduce the speed of SSDs. Most SSDs are typically split into 4 KiB sectors, while earlier systems may be based on 512 byte sectors with their default partition setups unaligned to the 4 KiB boundaries.^([\[194\]](#cite_note-1TeKf-194)) Windows Vista does not send the TRIM command to solid-state drives, but some third-party utilities such as SSD Doctor will periodically scan the drive and TRIM the appropriate entries.^([\[195\]](#cite_note-12toS-195))

#### Windows 7

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=41 "Edit section: Windows 7")\]

Windows 7 and later versions have native support for SSDs.^([\[189\]](#cite_note-:1-189)[\[196\]](#cite_note-tMARM-196)) The operating system detects the presence of an SSD and optimizes operation accordingly. For SSD devices, Windows 7 disables [ReadyBoost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ReadyBoost "ReadyBoost") and automatic defragmentation.^([\[197\]](#cite_note-197)) Despite the initial statement by Steven Sinofsky before the release of Windows 7,^([\[189\]](#cite_note-:1-189)) however, defragmentation is not disabled, even though its behavior on SSDs differs.^([\[198\]](#cite_note-:0-198)) One reason is the low performance of [Volume Shadow Copy Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume_Shadow_Copy_Service "Volume Shadow Copy Service") on fragmented SSDs.^([\[198\]](#cite_note-:0-198)) The second reason is to avoid reaching the practical maximum number of file fragments that a volume can handle.^([\[198\]](#cite_note-:0-198))

Windows 7 also includes support for the TRIM command to reduce garbage collection for data that the operating system has already determined is no longer valid.^([\[199\]](#cite_note-XTKn7-199)[\[200\]](#cite_note-0aQpS-200))

#### Windows 8.1 and later

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=42 "Edit section: Windows 8.1 and later")\]

Windows 8.1 and later Windows systems also support automatic TRIM for PCI Express SSDs based on NVMe. For Windows 7, the KB2990941 update is required for this functionality and needs to be integrated into Windows Setup using DISM if Windows 7 has to be installed on the NVMe SSD. Windows 8/8.1 also supports the SCSI unmap command, an analog of SATA TRIM, for USB-attached SSDs or SATA-to-USB enclosures. It is also supported over [USB Attached SCSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USB_Attached_SCSI "USB Attached SCSI") Protocol (UASP).

While Windows 7 supported automatic TRIM for internal SATA SSDs, Windows 8.1 and above support manual TRIM as well as automatic TRIM for SATA, NVMe and USB-attached SSDs. Manual TRIM is accessed through the expanded [Windows Defrag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_Defrag "Windows Defrag") utility.^([\[191\]](#cite_note-defrag8-191))

### ZFS

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=43 "Edit section: ZFS")\]

[Solaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solaris_(operating_system) "Solaris (operating system)") as of version 10 Update 6 (released in October 2008), and recent^(\[*[when?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Dates_and_numbers#Chronological_items "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Dates and numbers")*\]) versions of [OpenSolaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenSolaris "OpenSolaris"), [Solaris Express Community Edition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solaris_(operating_system)#SXCE "Solaris (operating system)"), [Illumos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illumos "Illumos"), [Linux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linux "Linux") with [ZFS on Linux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZFS#Native_ZFS_on_Linux "ZFS"), and [FreeBSD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FreeBSD "FreeBSD") all can use SSDs as a performance booster for [ZFS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZFS "ZFS"). A low-latency SSD can be used for the ZFS Intent Log (ZIL), where it is named the SLOG. An SSD may also be used for the level 2 [Adaptive Replacement Cache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptive_Replacement_Cache "Adaptive Replacement Cache") (L2ARC), which is used to cache data for reading.^([\[201\]](#cite_note-brendan_entry_test-201))

### FreeBSD

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid-state_drive&action=edit&section=44 "Edit section: FreeBSD")\]

ZFS for FreeBSD introduced support for TRIM on September 23, 2012.^([\[202\]](#cite_note-RbueE-202)) The [Unix File System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unix_File_System "Unix File System") also supports the TRIM command.^([\[203\]](#cite_note-TfqcL-203))
