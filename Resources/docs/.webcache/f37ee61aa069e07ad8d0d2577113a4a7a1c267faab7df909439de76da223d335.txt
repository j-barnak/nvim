A **hard disk drive** (**HDD**), **hard disk**, **hard drive**, or **fixed disk**^([\[a\]](#cite_note-1)) is an electro-mechanical [computer data storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_data_storage "Computer data storage") device that stores and retrieves [digital data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_data "Digital data") using [magnetic storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_storage "Magnetic storage") with one or more rigid rapidly rotating [platters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_drive_platter "Hard disk drive platter") coated with magnetic material. The platters are paired with [magnetic heads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_read-and-write_head "Disk read-and-write head"), usually arranged on a moving [actuator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actuator "Actuator") arm, which read and write data to the platter surfaces.^([\[1\]](#cite_note-ostep-2)) Data is accessed in a [random-access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random-access "Random-access") manner, meaning that individual [blocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block_(data_storage) "Block (data storage)") of data can be stored and retrieved in any order. HDDs are a type of [non-volatile storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-volatile_storage "Non-volatile storage"), retaining stored data when powered off.^([\[2\]](#cite_note-3)[\[3\]](#cite_note-4)[\[4\]](#cite_note-5)) Modern HDDs are typically in the form of a small rectangular box, possibly in a [disk enclosure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_enclosure "Disk enclosure") for portability. Modern disks do not have removable media, in contrast to the disk drives common in the 1960s and 1970s which often used removable [disk packs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_pack "Disk pack").^([\[b\]](#cite_note-6))

[IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM "IBM") introduced the first disk drive, the [350](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_350 "IBM 350") for the [305 RAMAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_305_RAMAC "IBM 305 RAMAC"), in 1956,^([\[5\]](#cite_note-IBM350-7)) and disks were the dominant [secondary storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_storage "Secondary storage") device for [general-purpose computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_general-purpose_CPUs "History of general-purpose CPUs") beginning in the early 1960s. HDDs maintained this position into the modern era of [servers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Server_(computing) "Server (computing)") and [personal computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_computer "Personal computer"), though personal computing devices produced in large volume, like [mobile phones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_phone "Mobile phone") and [tablets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tablet_computer "Tablet computer"), rely on [flash memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_memory "Flash memory") storage devices. More than 224 companies have [produced HDDs historically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_defunct_hard_disk_manufacturers "List of defunct hard disk manufacturers"), though after extensive industry consolidation, most units are manufactured by [Seagate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seagate_Technology "Seagate Technology"), [Toshiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toshiba "Toshiba"), and [Western Digital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Digital "Western Digital"). HDDs dominate the volume of storage produced ([exabytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exabyte "Exabyte") per year) for servers. Though production is growing slowly (by exabytes shipped^([\[6\]](#cite_note-8))), sales revenues and unit shipments are declining, because [solid-state drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid-state_drive "Solid-state drive") (SSDs) have higher data-transfer rates, higher areal storage density, somewhat better reliability,^([\[7\]](#cite_note-BBRel2021-9)[\[8\]](#cite_note-10)) and much lower latency and access times.^([\[9\]](#cite_note-11)[\[10\]](#cite_note-12)[\[11\]](#cite_note-arstechnica.com-13)[\[12\]](#cite_note-Santo_Domingo-14))

The revenues for SSDs, most of which use [NAND flash memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAND_flash_memory "NAND flash memory"), slightly exceeded those for HDDs in 2018.^([\[13\]](#cite_note-Barrons-15)) Flash storage products had more than twice the revenue of hard disk drives as of 2017^([\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit)).^([\[14\]](#cite_note-Register_UK_2017-16)) Though SSDs have four to nine times higher cost per bit,^([\[15\]](#cite_note-jcmit_2019-17)[\[16\]](#cite_note-blocks_August2019-18)) they are replacing HDDs in applications where speed, power consumption, small size, high capacity and durability are important.^([\[11\]](#cite_note-arstechnica.com-13)[\[12\]](#cite_note-Santo_Domingo-14)) As of 2017^([\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit)), the cost per bit of SSDs was falling, and the price premium over HDDs had narrowed.^([\[16\]](#cite_note-blocks_August2019-18))

The primary characteristics of an HDD are its capacity and [performance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_drive_performance_characteristics "Hard disk drive performance characteristics"). Capacity is specified in [unit prefixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_prefix "Unit prefix") corresponding to powers of 1000: a 1-[terabyte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terabyte "Terabyte") (TB) drive has a capacity of 1,000 [gigabytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gigabyte "Gigabyte"), where 1 gigabyte = 1 000 megabytes = 1 000 000 kilobytes (1 million) = 1 000 000 000 [bytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byte "Byte") (1 billion). Typically, some of an HDD's capacity is unavailable to the user because it is used by the [file system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File_system "File system") and the computer [operating system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operating_system "Operating system"), and possibly inbuilt redundancy for error correction and recovery. There can be confusion regarding storage capacity since capacities are stated in decimal gigabytes (powers of 1000) by HDD manufacturers, whereas the most commonly used operating systems report capacities in powers of 1024, which results in a smaller number than advertised. Performance is specified as the time required to move the heads to a track or cylinder (average access time), the time it takes for the desired sector to move under the head (average [latency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latency_(engineering) "Latency (engineering)"), which is a function of the physical [rotational speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotational_speed "Rotational speed") in [revolutions per minute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_per_minute "Revolutions per minute")), and finally, the speed at which the data is transmitted (data rate).

The two most common form factors for modern HDDs are 3.5-inch, for desktop computers, and 2.5-inch, primarily for laptops and servers. HDDs are connected to systems by standard [interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_interface "Computer interface") cables such as [SATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SATA "SATA") (Serial ATA), [USB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USB "USB"), SAS ([Serial Attached SCSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_Attached_SCSI "Serial Attached SCSI")), or [PATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_ATA "Parallel ATA") (Parallel ATA) cables.

### 1950s–1960s

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: 1950s–1960s")\]

The first production IBM hard disk drive, the [350 disk storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_350 "IBM 350"), shipped in 1957 as a component of the IBM 305 RAMAC system. It was approximately the size of two large refrigerators and stored five million six-bit characters (3.75 [megabytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megabyte "Megabyte"))^([\[18\]](#cite_note-350Cap-21)) on a stack of 52 disks (100 surfaces used).^([\[28\]](#cite_note-ibm-350-dsu-40)) The 350 had a single arm with two read/write heads, one facing up and the other down, that moved both horizontally between a pair of adjacent platters and vertically from one pair of platters to a second set.^([\[29\]](#cite_note-ibm-650-41)[\[30\]](#cite_note-ibm-7070-42)[\[31\]](#cite_note-ibm-1401-43)) Variants of the IBM 350 were the [IBM 355](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_355 "IBM 355"), [IBM 7300](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_7300 "IBM 7300") and [IBM 1405](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_1405 "IBM 1405").

In 1961, IBM announced, and in 1962 shipped, the IBM 1301 disk storage unit,^([\[32\]](#cite_note-44)) which superseded the IBM 350 and similar drives. The 1301 consisted of one (for Model 1) or two (for model 2) modules, each containing 25 platters, each platter about 1⁄8-inch (3.2 mm) thick and 24 inches (610 mm) in diameter.^([\[33\]](#cite_note-45)) While the earlier IBM disk drives used only two read/write heads per arm, the 1301 used an array of 48^([\[m\]](#cite_note-46)) heads (comb), each array moving horizontally as a single unit, one head per surface used. [Cylinder-mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder-head-sector "Cylinder-head-sector") read/write operations were supported, and the heads flew about 250 micro-inches (about 6 μm) above the platter surface. Motion of the head array depended upon a binary adder system of hydraulic actuators which assured repeatable positioning. The 1301 cabinet was about the size of three large refrigerators placed side by side, storing the equivalent of about 21 million eight-bit bytes per module. Access time was about a quarter of a second.

Also in 1962, IBM introduced the [model 1311](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_1311 "IBM 1311") disk drive, which was about the size of a washing machine and stored two million characters on a removable [disk pack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_pack "Disk pack"). Users could buy additional packs and interchange them as needed, much like reels of [magnetic tape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_tape "Magnetic tape"). Later models of removable pack drives, from IBM and others, became the norm in most computer installations and reached capacities of 300 megabytes by the early 1980s. Non-removable HDDs were called "fixed disk" drives.

In 1963, IBM introduced the 1302,^([\[34\]](#cite_note-1301Ref-47)) with twice the track capacity and twice as many tracks per cylinder as the 1301. The 1302 had one (for Model 1) or two (for Model 2) modules, each containing a separate comb for the first 250 tracks and the last 250 tracks.

Some high-performance HDDs were manufactured with one head per track, e.g., the Burroughs B-475 in 1964 and the [IBM 2305](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_IBM_magnetic_disk_drives#IBM_2305 "History of IBM magnetic disk drives") in 1970, so that no time was lost physically moving the heads to a track and the only latency was the time for the desired block of data to rotate into position under the head.^([\[35\]](#cite_note-48)) Known as fixed-head or head-per-track disk drives, they were very expensive and are no longer in production.^([\[36\]](#cite_note-CHAUDHURI2008-49))

### 1970s

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: 1970s")\]

In 1973, IBM introduced a new type of HDD code-named "[Winchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_3340 "IBM 3340")". Its primary distinguishing feature was that the disk heads were not withdrawn completely from the stack of disk platters when the drive was powered down. Instead, the heads were allowed to "land" on a special area of the disk surface upon spin-down, "taking off" again when the disk was later powered on. This greatly reduced the cost of the head actuator mechanism but precluded removing just the disks from the drive as was done with the disk packs of the day. Instead, the first models of "Winchester technology" drives featured a removable disk module, which included both the disk pack and the head assembly, leaving the actuator motor in the drive upon removal. Later "Winchester" drives abandoned the removable media concept and returned to non-removable platters.

In 1974, IBM introduced the swinging arm actuator, made feasible because the Winchester recording heads function well when skewed to the recorded tracks. The simple design of the IBM GV (Gulliver) drive,^([\[37\]](#cite_note-50)) invented at IBM's UK Hursley Labs, became IBM's most licensed electro-mechanical invention^([\[38\]](#cite_note-51)) of all time, the actuator and filtration system being adopted in the 1980s eventually for all HDDs, and still universal over 50 years and 10 billion arms later.

Like the first removable pack drive, the first "Winchester" drives used platters 14 inches (360 mm) in diameter. In 1978, IBM introduced a swing arm drive, the IBM 0680 (Piccolo), with eight-inch platters, exploring the possibility that smaller platters might offer advantages. Other eight-inch drives followed, then 5+1⁄4 in (130 mm) drives, sized to replace the contemporary [floppy disk drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floppy_disk_drive "Floppy disk drive"). The latter were primarily intended for the then fledgling personal computer (PC) market.

### 1980s–1990s

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: 1980s–1990s")\]

Over time, as recording densities were greatly increased, further reductions in disk diameter to 3.5" and 2.5" were found to be optimum. Powerful rare-earth magnet materials became affordable during this period and were complementary to the swing arm actuator design to make possible the compact form factors of modern HDDs.

By 1986, 10 MB was the most commonly used hard disk size, and most new corporate computers had hard drives. Most in the industry expected that 20 MB would soon become the new standard size.^([\[39\]](#cite_note-welch19860616-52)) By the late 1980s, their cost had been reduced to the point where they were standard on all but the cheapest computers.

Most HDDs in the early 1980s were sold to PC end users as an external, add-on subsystem. The subsystem was not sold under the drive manufacturer's name but under the subsystem manufacturer's name such as [Corvus Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corvus_Systems "Corvus Systems") and [Tallgrass Technologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tallgrass_Technologies "Tallgrass Technologies"), or under the PC system manufacturer's name such as the [Apple ProFile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_ProFile "Apple ProFile").

The [IBM PC XT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_PC_XT "IBM PC XT") in 1983 included an internal 10 MB HDD; by 1985 internal HDDs outsold external drives for personal computers. When IBM began selling the XT without a hard disk, hard disk manufacturers that had only sold to [OEM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OEM "OEM") customers began selling to resellers, which bundled XTs with the non-IBM hard disks. [Hardcards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardcard "Hardcard") became popular upgrades for existing computers.^([\[39\]](#cite_note-welch19860616-52))

External HDDs remained popular for much longer on the [Apple Macintosh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Macintosh "Apple Macintosh"). Many Macintosh computers made between 1986 and 1998 featured a [SCSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SCSI "SCSI") port on the back, making external expansion simple. Older compact Macintosh computers did not have user-accessible hard drive bays (indeed, the [Macintosh 128K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macintosh_128K "Macintosh 128K"), [Macintosh 512K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macintosh_512K "Macintosh 512K"), and [Macintosh Plus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macintosh_Plus "Macintosh Plus") did not feature a hard drive bay at all), so on those models, external SCSI disks were the only reasonable option for expanding upon any internal storage.

### 21st century

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: 21st century")\]

HDD improvements have been driven by increasing [areal density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_(computer_storage) "Density (computer storage)"), listed in the table above. Applications expanded through the 2000s, from the [mainframe computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainframe_computer "Mainframe computer") of the late 1950s to most [mass storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_storage "Mass storage") applications including computers and consumer applications such as storage of entertainment content.

In the 2000s and 2010s, NAND began supplanting HDDs in applications requiring portability or high performance. In 2018, the largest hard drive had a capacity of 15 TB, while the largest capacity SSD had a capacity of 100 TB.^([\[40\]](#cite_note-100TB_2018-53)) In 2018, HDDs were forecast to reach 100 TB capacities around 2025,^([\[41\]](#cite_note-54)) but as of 2019^([\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit)), the expected pace of improvement was pared back to 50 TB by 2026.^([\[42\]](#cite_note-blocks_WWrevenue_August2019-55)) Smaller form factors (1.8 inches and below) were discontinued around 2010.

The cost of solid-state storage (NAND), represented by [Moore's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore's_law "Moore's law"), is improving faster than HDDs. NAND has a higher [price elasticity of demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_elasticity_of_demand "Price elasticity of demand") than HDDs, and this drives market growth.^([\[43\]](#cite_note-Appleton-56)) During the late 2000s and 2010s, the [product life cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marketing#Product_life_cycle "Marketing") of HDDs entered a mature phase, and slowing sales may indicate the onset of the declining phase.^([\[44\]](#cite_note-lifecycle-57)) During the 2020s, NAND adoption rate has significantly accelerated, in no small part due to the rising use of the technology in consumer electronics, while its performance has been improving faster than HDD performance.

The [2011 Thailand floods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Thailand_floods "2011 Thailand floods") damaged the manufacturing plants and impacted hard disk drive cost adversely between 2011 and 2013.^([\[45\]](#cite_note-58))

In 2019, [Western Digital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Digital "Western Digital") closed its last Malaysian HDD factory due to decreasing demand, to focus on SSD production.^([\[46\]](#cite_note-59)) All three remaining HDD manufacturers have had decreasing demand for their HDDs since 2014.^([\[47\]](#cite_note-60))

## Technology

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Technology")\]

### Magnetic recording

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Magnetic recording")\]

Video overview of how HDDs work

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/33/2019_Rozebrane_dyski_twarde.jpg/250px-2019_Rozebrane_dyski_twarde.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:2019_Rozebrane_dyski_twarde.jpg)

A 3.5" and a 2.5" HDD with front covers removed to show their internals

See also: [Magnetic storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_storage "Magnetic storage")

A modern HDD records data by magnetizing a thin film of [ferromagnetic material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetic_material "Ferromagnetic material")^([\[n\]](#cite_note-62)) on both sides of a disk. Sequential changes in the direction of magnetization represent binary data [bits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit "Bit"). The data is read from the disk by detecting the transitions in magnetization. User data is encoded using an encoding scheme, such as [run-length limited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Run-length_limited "Run-length limited") encoding,^([\[o\]](#cite_note-63)) which determines how the data is represented by the magnetic transitions.

A typical HDD design consists of a *spindle* that holds flat circular disks, called [platters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_drive_platter "Hard disk drive platter"), which hold the recorded data. The platters are made from a non-magnetic material, usually [aluminum alloy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminum_alloy "Aluminum alloy"), [glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass "Glass"), or [ceramic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceramic "Ceramic"). They are coated with a shallow layer of magnetic material typically 10–20 [nm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanometer "Nanometer") in depth, with an outer layer of carbon for protection.^([\[49\]](#cite_note-headcrash-64)[\[50\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-6-65)[\[51\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-7-66)) For reference, a standard piece of copy paper is 0.07–0.18 mm (70,000–180,000 nm)^([\[52\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-8-67)) thick.

The platters in contemporary HDDs are spun at speeds varying from 4200 [rpm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_per_minute "Revolutions per minute") in energy-efficient portable devices, to 15,000 rpm for high-performance servers.^([\[53\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-10-68)) The first HDDs spun at 1,200 rpm^([\[5\]](#cite_note-IBM350-7)) and, for many years, 3,600 rpm was the norm.^([\[54\]](#cite_note-69)) As of November 2019^([\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit)), the platters in most consumer-grade HDDs spin at 5,400 or 7,200 rpm.

Information is written to and read from a platter as it rotates past devices called [read-and-write heads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_read-and-write_head "Disk read-and-write head") that are positioned to operate very close to the magnetic surface, with their [flying height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flying_height "Flying height") often in the range of tens of nanometers. The read-and-write head is used to detect and modify the magnetization of the material passing immediately under it.

In modern drives, there is one head for each magnetic platter surface on the spindle, mounted on a common arm. An actuator arm (or access arm) moves the heads on an arc (roughly radially) across the platters as they spin, allowing each head to access almost the entire surface of the platter as it spins. The arm is moved using a [voice coil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice_coil "Voice coil") actuator or, in some older designs, a [stepper motor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stepper_motor "Stepper motor"). Early hard disk drives wrote data at some constant bits per second, resulting in all tracks having the same amount of data per track, but modern drives (since the 1990s) use [zone bit recording](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zone_bit_recording "Zone bit recording"), increasing the write speed from inner to outer zone and thereby storing more data per track in the outer zones.

In modern drives, the small size of the magnetic regions creates the danger that their magnetic state might be lost because of [thermal effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superparamagnetism "Superparamagnetism")⁠ ⁠— thermally induced magnetic instability which is commonly known as the "[superparamagnetic limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superparamagnetic_limit "Superparamagnetic limit")". To counter this, the platters are coated with two parallel magnetic layers, separated by a three-atom layer of the non-magnetic element [ruthenium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruthenium "Ruthenium"), and the two layers are magnetized in opposite orientation, thus reinforcing each other.^([\[55\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-15-70)) Another technology used to overcome thermal effects to allow greater recording densities is [perpendicular recording](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpendicular_recording "Perpendicular recording") (PMR), first shipped in 2005,^([\[56\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-16-71)) and as of 2007^([\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit)), used in certain HDDs.^([\[57\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-17-72)[\[58\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-18-73)[\[59\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-19-74)) Perpendicular recording may be accompanied by changes in the manufacturing of the read/write heads to increase the strength of the magnetic field created by the heads.^([\[60\]](#cite_note-75))

In 2004, a higher-density recording media was introduced, consisting of coupled soft and hard magnetic layers. So-called *[exchange spring media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_spring_media "Exchange spring media")* magnetic storage technology, also known as *exchange coupled composite media*, allows good writability due to the write-assist nature of the soft layer. However, the thermal stability is determined only by the hardest layer and not influenced by the soft layer.^([\[61\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-19a-76)[\[62\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-19b-77))

Flux control MAMR (FC-MAMR) allows a hard drive to have increased recording capacity without the need for new hard disk drive platter materials. MAMR hard drives have a microwave-generating spin torque generator (STO) on the read/write heads which allows physically smaller bits to be recorded to the platters, increasing areal density. Normally hard drive recording heads have a pole called a main pole that is used for writing to the platters, and adjacent to this pole is an air gap and a shield. The write coil of the head surrounds the pole. The STO device is placed in the air gap between the pole and the shield to increase the strength of the magnetic field created by the pole; FC-MAMR technically doesn't use microwaves but uses technology employed in MAMR. The STO has a Field Generation Layer (FGL) and a Spin Injection Layer (SIL), and the FGL produces a magnetic field using spin-polarised electrons originating in the SIL, which is a form of spin torque energy.^([\[63\]](#cite_note-78))

### Components

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Components")\]

The *actuator* is a [permanent magnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permanent_magnet "Permanent magnet") and [moving coil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moving_coil "Moving coil") motor that swings the heads to the desired position. The head support arm is very light, but also stiff; in modern drives, acceleration at the head reaches 550 [*g*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G-force "G-force"). A metal plate supports a squat [neodymium–iron–boron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neodymium_magnet "Neodymium magnet") (NIB) high-flux [magnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnet "Magnet"). Beneath this plate is the moving coil, often referred to as the *[voice coil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice_coil "Voice coil")* by analogy to the coil in [loudspeakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loudspeaker "Loudspeaker"), which is attached to the actuator hub, and beneath that is a second NIB magnet, mounted on the bottom plate of the motor (some drives have only one magnet).

The voice coil itself is shaped rather like an arrowhead and is made of doubly coated copper [magnet wire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnet_wire "Magnet wire"). The inner layer is insulation, and the outer is thermoplastic, which bonds the coil together after it is wound on a form, making it self-supporting. The portions of the coil along the two sides of the arrowhead (which point to the center of the actuator bearing) then interact with the [magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field "Magnetic field") of the fixed magnet. Current flowing radially outward along one side of the arrowhead and radially inward on the other produces the [tangential force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field#Force_on_a_charged_particle "Magnetic field"). If the magnetic field were uniform, each side would generate opposing forces that would cancel each other out. Therefore, the surface of the magnet is half north pole and half south pole, with the radial dividing line in the middle, causing the two sides of the coil to see opposite magnetic fields and produce forces that add instead of canceling. Currents along the top and bottom of the coil produce radial forces that do not rotate the head.

The HDD's electronics controls the movement of the actuator and the rotation of the disk and transfers data to or from a [disk controller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_controller "Disk controller"). Feedback of the drive electronics is accomplished by means of special segments of the disk dedicated to [servo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servomotor "Servomotor") feedback. These are either complete concentric circles (in the case of dedicated servo technology) or segments interspersed with real data (in the case of embedded servo, otherwise known as sector servo technology). The servo feedback optimizes the signal-to-noise ratio of the GMR sensors by adjusting the voice coil motor to rotate the arm. A more modern servo system also employs milli or micro actuators to more accurately position the read/write heads.^([\[65\]](#cite_note-80)) The spinning of the disks uses fluid-bearing spindle motors. Modern disk firmware is capable of scheduling reads and writes efficiently on the platter surfaces and remapping sectors of the media that have failed.

###  Error rates and handling

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section:  Error rates and handling")\]

Modern drives make extensive use of [error correction codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_correction_code "Error correction code") (ECCs), particularly [Reed–Solomon error correction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reed–Solomon_error_correction "Reed–Solomon error correction"). These techniques store extra bits, determined by mathematical formulas, for each block of data; the extra bits allow many errors to be corrected invisibly. The extra bits themselves take up space on the HDD, but allow higher recording densities to be employed without causing uncorrectable errors, resulting in much larger storage capacity.^([\[66\]](#cite_note-81)) For example, a typical 1 [TB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terabyte "Terabyte") hard disk with 512-byte sectors provides additional capacity of about 93 [GB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibibyte "Gibibyte") for the [ECC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forward_error_correction "Forward error correction") data.^([\[67\]](#cite_note-82))

In the newest drives, as of 2009^([\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit)),^([\[68\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-21-83)) [low-density parity-check codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-density_parity-check_code "Low-density parity-check code") (LDPC) were supplanting Reed–Solomon; LDPC codes enable performance close to the [Shannon limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_limit "Shannon limit") and thus provide the highest storage density available.^([\[68\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-21-83)[\[69\]](#cite_note-84))

Typical hard disk drives attempt to "remap" the data in a [physical sector that is failing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_sector "Bad sector") to a spare physical sector provided by the drive's "spare sector pool" (also called "reserve pool"),^([\[70\]](#cite_note-85)) while relying on the ECC to recover stored data while the number of errors in a bad sector is still low enough. The S.M.A.R.T ([Self-Monitoring, Analysis and Reporting Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-Monitoring,_Analysis_and_Reporting_Technology "Self-Monitoring, Analysis and Reporting Technology")) feature counts the total number of errors in the entire HDD fixed by ECC (although not on all hard drives as the related S.M.A.R.T attributes "Hardware ECC Recovered" and "Soft ECC Correction" are not consistently supported), and the total number of performed sector remappings, as the occurrence of many such errors may predict an [HDD failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HDD_failure "HDD failure").

The "No-ID Format", developed by IBM in the mid-1990s, contains information about which sectors are bad and where remapped sectors have been located.^([\[71\]](#cite_note-86))

Only a tiny fraction of the detected errors end up as not correctable. Examples of specified uncorrected bit read error rates include:

- 2013 specifications for enterprise SAS disk drives state the error rate to be one uncorrected bit read error in every 10¹⁶ bits read,^([\[72\]](#cite_note-SGAT2013-87)[\[73\]](#cite_note-WD2013-88))
- 2018 specifications for consumer SATA hard drives state the error rate to be one uncorrected bit read error in every 10¹⁴ bits.^([\[74\]](#cite_note-SGAT2018-89)[\[75\]](#cite_note-WD2018-90))

Within a given manufacturers model the uncorrected bit error rate is typically the same regardless of capacity of the drive.^([\[72\]](#cite_note-SGAT2013-87)[\[73\]](#cite_note-WD2013-88)[\[74\]](#cite_note-SGAT2018-89)[\[75\]](#cite_note-WD2018-90))

The worst type of errors are [silent data corruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silent_data_corruption "Silent data corruption") which are errors undetected by the disk firmware or the host operating system; some of these errors may be caused by hard disk drive malfunctions while others originate elsewhere in the connection between the drive and the host.^([\[76\]](#cite_note-91))

###  Development

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section:  Development")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b9/Full_History_Disk_Areal_Density_Trend.png/250px-Full_History_Disk_Areal_Density_Trend.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Full_History_Disk_Areal_Density_Trend.png)

Leading-edge hard disk drive [areal densities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_(computer_storage) "Density (computer storage)") from 1956 through 2009 compared to [Moore's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore's_law "Moore's law"). By 2016, progress had slowed significantly below the extrapolated density trend.^([\[77\]](#cite_note-Hayes_2016-92))

The rate of areal density advancement was similar to [Moore's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore's_law "Moore's law") (doubling every two years) through 2010: 60% per year during 1988–1996, 100% during 1996–2003 and 30% during 2003–2010.^([\[78\]](#cite_note-Byrne2015b-93)) Speaking in 1997, [Gordon Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Moore "Gordon Moore") called the increase "flabbergasting",^([\[79\]](#cite_note-Moore1997-94)) while observing later that growth cannot continue forever.^([\[80\]](#cite_note-Moore2005-95)) Price improvement decelerated to −12% per year during 2010–2017,^([\[81\]](#cite_note-McCallum-96)) as the growth of areal density slowed. The rate of advancement for areal density slowed to 10% per year during 2010–2016,^([\[82\]](#cite_note-IBM_Fontana_2016-97)) and there was difficulty in migrating from perpendicular recording to newer technologies.^([\[83\]](#cite_note-Mellor_2014-11-10-98))

As bit cell size decreases, more data can be put onto a single drive platter. In 2013, a production desktop 3 TB HDD (with four platters) would have had an areal density of about 500 Gbit/in² which would have amounted to a bit cell comprising about 18 magnetic grains (11 by 1.6 grains).^([\[84\]](#cite_note-Anderson2013a-99)) Since the mid-2000s, areal density progress has been challenged by a [superparamagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superparamagnetism#Effect_on_hard_drives "Superparamagnetism") trilemma involving grain size, grain magnetic strength and ability of the head to write.^([\[85\]](#cite_note-100)) In order to maintain acceptable signal-to-noise, smaller grains are required; smaller grains may self-reverse ([electrothermal instability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrothermal_instability "Electrothermal instability")) unless their magnetic strength is increased, but known write head materials are unable to generate a strong enough magnetic field sufficient to write the medium in the increasingly smaller space taken by grains.

Magnetic storage technologies are being developed to address this trilemma, and compete with [flash memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_memory "Flash memory")–based [solid-state drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid-state_drive "Solid-state drive") (SSDs). In 2013, [Seagate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seagate_Technology "Seagate Technology") introduced [shingled magnetic recording](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shingled_magnetic_recording "Shingled magnetic recording") (SMR),^([\[86\]](#cite_note-Seagate_2013-09-09-101)) intended as something of a "stopgap" technology between PMR and Seagate's intended successor [heat-assisted magnetic recording](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat-assisted_magnetic_recording "Heat-assisted magnetic recording") (HAMR). SMR utilizes overlapping tracks for increased data density, at the cost of design complexity and lower data access speeds (particularly write speeds and [random access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_access "Random access") 4k speeds).^([\[87\]](#cite_note-102)[\[88\]](#cite_note-103))

By contrast, [HGST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HGST "HGST") (now part of [Western Digital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Digital "Western Digital")) focused on developing ways to seal [helium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium "Helium")-filled drives instead of the usual filtered air. Since [turbulence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbulence "Turbulence") and [friction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friction "Friction") are reduced, higher areal densities can be achieved due to using a smaller track width, and the energy dissipated due to friction is lower as well, resulting in a lower power draw. Furthermore, more platters can be fit into the same enclosure space, although helium gas is notoriously difficult to prevent escaping.^([\[89\]](#cite_note-104)) Thus, helium drives are completely sealed and do not have a breather port, unlike their air-filled counterparts. They have two top covers, the second is laser welded in place and is the only one visible from the exterior.^([\[90\]](#cite_note-105))

Other recording technologies are either under research or have been commercially implemented to increase areal density, including Seagate's [heat-assisted magnetic recording](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat-assisted_magnetic_recording "Heat-assisted magnetic recording") (HAMR). HAMR requires a different architecture with redesigned media and read/write heads, new lasers, and new near-field optical transducers.^([\[91\]](#cite_note-Shilov_anandtech_b-106)) HAMR shipped commercially in early 2024^([\[92\]](#cite_note-107)) after technical issues delayed its introduction by more than a decade, from earlier projections as early as 2009.^([\[93\]](#cite_note-HAMR_2008_for_2009-108)[\[94\]](#cite_note-HAMR_2014_for_2015-109)[\[95\]](#cite_note-HAMR_2013_for_2016-110)[\[96\]](#cite_note-blocks_HAMR_2019-111)) HAMR's planned successor, [bit-patterned recording](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit-patterned_recording "Bit-patterned recording") (BPR),^([\[97\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-22-112)) has been removed from the roadmaps of Western Digital and Seagate.^([\[98\]](#cite_note-BPR_roadmaps_2018-113)) Western Digital's microwave-assisted magnetic recording (MAMR),^([\[99\]](#cite_note-114)[\[100\]](#cite_note-115)) also referred to as energy-assisted magnetic recording (EAMR), was sampled in 2020, with the first EAMR drive, the Ultrastar HC550, shipping in late 2020.^([\[101\]](#cite_note-116)[\[102\]](#cite_note-Blocks_MAMR_2019-117)[\[103\]](#cite_note-118)) [Two-dimensional magnetic recording](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-dimensional_magnetic_recording "Two-dimensional magnetic recording") (TDMR)^([\[84\]](#cite_note-Anderson2013a-99)[\[104\]](#cite_note-Wood_2010-119)) and "current perpendicular to plane" [giant magnetoresistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant_magnetoresistance "Giant magnetoresistance") (CPP/GMR) heads have appeared in research papers.^([\[105\]](#cite_note-Coughlin-120)[\[106\]](#cite_note-121)[\[107\]](#cite_note-122))

Some drives have adopted dual independent actuator arms to increase read/write speeds and compete with SSDs.^([\[108\]](#cite_note-123)) A 3D-actuated vacuum drive (3DHD) concept^([\[109\]](#cite_note-3DHD_blog-124)) and 3D magnetic recording have been proposed.^([\[110\]](#cite_note-125))

Depending upon assumptions on feasibility and timing of these technologies, Seagate forecasts that areal density will grow 20% per year during 2020–2034.^([\[42\]](#cite_note-blocks_WWrevenue_August2019-55))

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d0/MSATA_SSD_vs._2.5%22_SATA_drive.JPG/250px-MSATA_SSD_vs._2.5%22_SATA_drive.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:MSATA_SSD_vs._2.5%22_SATA_drive.JPG)

The highest-capacity HDDs shipping commercially as of 2025^([\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit)) are 36 TB.^([\[20\]](#cite_note-x-mozaic-23))

The capacity of a hard disk drive, as reported by an operating system to the end user, is smaller than the amount stated by the manufacturer for several reasons, e.g. the operating system using some space, use of some space for data redundancy, space use for file system structures. Confusion of [decimal prefixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_prefix "Decimal prefix") and [binary prefixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_prefix "Binary prefix") can also lead to errors.

### Calculation

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Calculation")\]

Modern hard disk drives appear to their host controller as a contiguous set of logical blocks, and the gross drive capacity is calculated by multiplying the number of blocks by the block size. This information is available from the manufacturer's product specification, and from the drive itself through use of operating system functions that invoke low-level drive commands.^([\[111\]](#cite_note-SAS-126)[\[112\]](#cite_note-SATA-127)) Older IBM and compatible drives, e.g. [IBM 3390](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_3390 "IBM 3390") using the [CKD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count_key_data "Count key data") record format, have variable length records; such drive capacity calculations must take into account the characteristics of the records. Some newer DASD simulate CKD, and the same capacity formulae apply.

The gross capacity of older sector-oriented HDDs is calculated as the product of the number of [cylinders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_(disk_drive) "Cylinder (disk drive)") per recording zone, the number of bytes per sector (most commonly 512), and the count of [zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zone_bit_recording "Zone bit recording") of the drive.^(\[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*\]) Some modern SATA drives also report [cylinder-head-sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder-head-sector "Cylinder-head-sector") (CHS) capacities, but these are not physical parameters because the reported values are constrained by historic operating system interfaces. The C/H/S scheme has been replaced by [logical block addressing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_block_addressing "Logical block addressing") (LBA), a simple linear addressing scheme that locates blocks by an integer index, which starts at LBA 0 for the first block and increments thereafter.^([\[113\]](#cite_note-128)) When using the C/H/S method to describe modern large drives, the number of heads is often set to 64, although a typical modern hard disk drive has between one and four platters. In modern HDDs, spare capacity for [defect management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_hard-drive_defect_management "Internal hard-drive defect management") is not included in the published capacity; however, in many early HDDs, a certain number of sectors were reserved as spares, thereby reducing the capacity available to the operating system. Furthermore, many HDDs store their firmware in a reserved service zone, which is typically not accessible by the user, and is not included in the capacity calculation.

For [RAID](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAID "RAID") subsystems, [data integrity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_integrity "Data integrity") and fault-tolerance requirements also reduce the realized capacity. For example, a RAID 1 array has about half the total capacity as a result of data mirroring, while a RAID 5 array with n drives loses 1/n of capacity (which equals to the capacity of a single drive) due to storing parity information. RAID subsystems are multiple drives that appear to be one drive or more drives to the user, but provide fault tolerance. Most RAID vendors use [checksums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Checksum "Checksum") to improve data integrity at the block level. Some vendors design systems using HDDs with sectors of 520 bytes to contain 512 bytes of user data and eight checksum bytes, or by using separate 512-byte sectors for the checksum data.^([\[114\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-38-129))

Some systems may use [hidden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host_protected_area "Host protected area") [partitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_partitioning "Disk partitioning") for system recovery, reducing the capacity available to the end user without knowledge of [special disk partitioning utilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_disk_partitioning_software "List of disk partitioning software") like [diskpart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diskpart "Diskpart") in [Windows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows "Windows").^([\[115\]](#cite_note-130))

### Formatting

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Formatting")\]

Main article: [Disk formatting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_formatting "Disk formatting")

Data is stored on a hard drive in a series of logical blocks. Each block is delimited by markers identifying its start and end, error detecting and correcting information, and space between blocks to allow for minor timing variations. These blocks often contained 512 bytes of usable data, but other sizes have been used. As drive density increased, an initiative known as [Advanced Format](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Format "Advanced Format") extended the block size to 4096 bytes of usable data, with a resulting significant reduction in the amount of disk space used for block headers, error-checking data, and spacing.

The process of initializing these logical blocks on the physical disk platters is called *low-level formatting*, which is usually performed at the factory and is not normally changed in the field.^([\[116\]](#cite_note-131)) *High-level formatting* writes data structures used by the operating system to organize data files on the disk. This includes writing [partition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_partitioning "Disk partitioning") and [file system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File_system "File system") structures into selected logical blocks. For example, some of the disk space will be used to hold a directory of disk file names and a list of logical blocks associated with a particular file.

Examples of partition mapping scheme include [master boot record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_boot_record "Master boot record") (MBR) and [GUID Partition Table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GUID_Partition_Table "GUID Partition Table") (GPT). Examples of data structures stored on disk to retrieve files include the [File Allocation Table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File_Allocation_Table "File Allocation Table") (FAT) in the [MS-DOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MS-DOS "MS-DOS") file system and [inodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inode "Inode") in many [UNIX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNIX "UNIX") file systems, as well as other operating system data structures (also known as [metadata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metadata "Metadata")). As a consequence, not all the space on an HDD is available for user files, but this system overhead is usually small compared with user data.

In the early days of computing, the total capacity of HDDs was specified in seven to nine decimal digits frequently truncated with the idiom *millions*.^([\[119\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-26-136)[\[34\]](#cite_note-1301Ref-47)) By the 1970s, the total capacity of HDDs was given by manufacturers using [SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI "SI") decimal prefixes such as [megabytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megabyte "Megabyte") (1 MB = 1,000,000 bytes), [gigabytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gigabyte "Gigabyte") (1 GB = 1,000,000,000 bytes) and [terabytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terabyte "Terabyte") (1 TB = 1,000,000,000,000 bytes).^([\[117\]](#cite_note-SeagateStorageGuide-132)[\[120\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-27-137)[\[121\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-28-138)[\[122\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-29-139)) However, capacities of [memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_memory "Main memory") are usually quoted using a [binary interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_prefixes "Binary prefixes") of the prefixes, i.e. using powers of 1024 instead of 1000.

Software reports hard disk drive or memory capacity in different forms using either decimal or binary prefixes. The [Microsoft Windows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Windows "Microsoft Windows") family of operating systems uses the binary convention when reporting storage capacity, so an HDD offered by its manufacturer as a 1 TB drive is reported by these operating systems as a 931 GB HDD. [Mac OS X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mac_OS_X "Mac OS X") 10.6 ("[Snow Leopard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mac_OS_X_Snow_Leopard "Mac OS X Snow Leopard")") uses decimal convention when reporting HDD capacity.^([\[123\]](#cite_note-Apple-140)) The default behavior of the df [command-line utility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Command-line_utility "Command-line utility") on Linux is to report the HDD capacity as a number of 1024-byte units.^([\[124\]](#cite_note-141))

The difference between the decimal and binary prefix interpretation caused some consumer confusion and led to class action suits [against HDD manufacturers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_prefix#Legal_disputes "Binary prefix"). The plaintiffs argued that the use of decimal prefixes effectively misled consumers, while the defendants denied any wrongdoing or liability, asserting that their marketing and advertising complied in all respects with the law and that no class member sustained any damages or injuries.^([\[125\]](#cite_note-WDSettle-142)[\[126\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-30-143)[\[127\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-31-144)) In 2020, a California court ruled that use of the decimal prefixes with a decimal meaning was not misleading.^([\[128\]](#cite_note-145))

[![Portable hard drives in enclosures](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/76/WD_External_Hard_Drives_IMG_7899.jpg/250px-WD_External_Hard_Drives_IMG_7899.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:WD_External_Hard_Drives_IMG_7899.jpg)

Portable hard drives in enclosures

IBM's first hard disk drive, the [IBM 350](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_350 "IBM 350"), used a stack of fifty 24-inch platters, stored 3.75 MB of data (approximately the size of one modern digital picture), and was of a size comparable to two large refrigerators. In 1962, [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM "IBM") introduced its [model 1311](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_1311 "IBM 1311") disk, which used six 14-inch (nominal size) platters in a removable pack and was roughly the size of a washing machine. This became a standard platter size for many years, used also by other manufacturers.^([\[129\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-39-146)) The [IBM 2314](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_2314 "IBM 2314") used platters of the same size in an eleven-high pack and introduced the "drive in a drawer" layout, sometimes called the "pizza oven", although the "drawer" was not the complete drive. Into the 1970s, HDDs were offered in standalone cabinets of varying dimensions containing from one to four HDDs.

Beginning in the late 1960s, drives were offered that fit entirely into a chassis that would mount in a [19-inch rack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/19-inch_rack "19-inch rack"). Digital's [RK05](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RK05 "RK05") and RL01 were early examples using single 14-inch platters in removable packs, the entire drive fitting in a 10.5-inch-high rack space (six rack units). In the mid-to-late 1980s, the similarly sized [Fujitsu Eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fujitsu_Eagle "Fujitsu Eagle"), which used (coincidentally) 10.5-inch platters, was a popular product.

With increasing sales of microcomputers having built-in [floppy-disk drives (FDDs)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floppy_disk "Floppy disk"), HDDs that would fit to the FDD mountings became desirable. Starting with the [Shugart Associates SA1000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shugart_Associates "Shugart Associates"), HDD form factors initially followed those of 8-inch, 5+1⁄4-inch, and 3+1⁄2-inch floppy disk drives. Although referred to by these nominal sizes, the actual sizes for those three drives respectively are 9.5", 5.75" and 4" wide. Because there were no smaller floppy disk drives, smaller HDD form factors such as 2+1⁄2-inch drives (actually 2.75" wide) developed from product offerings or industry standards.

As of 2025^([\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit)), 2+1⁄2-inch and 3+1⁄2-inch hard disks are the most popular sizes. By 2009, all manufacturers had discontinued the development of new products for the 1.3-inch, 1-inch and 0.85-inch form factors due to falling prices of [flash memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_memory "Flash memory"),^([\[130\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-51-147)[\[131\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-52-148)) which has no moving parts. While nominal sizes are in inches, actual dimensions are specified in millimeters.

Consumer hard drives are commonly sold pre-packaged in [disk enclosures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_enclosure "Disk enclosure"), which protect the device and allow attaching them via common general purpose interfaces like USB, allowing the device to remain separate from any computer using it and to be portable. Such enclosures vary widely in size as they are usually not intended to be inserted into a system as a fixed component. Enclosures may also contain multiple hard drives combined as [RAID](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAID "RAID").

## Performance characteristics

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Performance characteristics")\]

Main article: [Hard disk drive performance characteristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_drive_performance_characteristics "Hard disk drive performance characteristics")

The factors that limit the [time to access the data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Access_time "Access time") on an HDD are mostly related to the mechanical nature of the rotating disks and moving heads, including:

- [Seek time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_drive_performance_characteristics#Seek_time "Hard disk drive performance characteristics") is a measure of how long it takes the head assembly to travel to the track of the disk that contains data.
- Rotational latency is incurred because the desired [disk sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_sector "Disk sector") may not be directly under the head when data transfer is requested. Average rotational latency is shown in the table, based on the statistical relation that the average latency is one-half the rotational period.
- The [bit rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit_rate "Bit rate") or data transfer rate (once the head is in the right position) creates delay which is a function of the number of blocks transferred; typically relatively small, but can be quite long with the transfer of large contiguous files.

Delay may also occur if the drive disks are stopped to save energy.

[Defragmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defragmentation "Defragmentation") is a procedure used to minimize delay in retrieving data by moving related items to physically proximate areas on the disk.^([\[132\]](#cite_note-itworld-2001-04-18-149)) Some computer operating systems perform defragmentation automatically. Although automatic defragmentation is intended to reduce access delays, performance will be temporarily reduced while the procedure is in progress.^([\[133\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-67-150))

Time to access data can be improved by increasing rotational speed (thus reducing latency) or by reducing the time spent seeking. Increasing areal density increases [throughput](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Throughput "Throughput") by increasing data rate and by increasing the amount of data under a set of heads, thereby potentially reducing seek activity for a given amount of data. The time to access data has not kept up with throughput increases, which themselves have not kept up with growth in bit density and storage capacity.

### Data transfer rate

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Data transfer rate")\]

As of 2010^([\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit)), a typical 7,200-rpm desktop HDD has a sustained "disk-to-[buffer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_buffer "Disk buffer")" data transfer rate up to 1,030 Mbit/s.^([\[134\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-71-152)) This rate depends on the track location; the rate is higher for data on the outer tracks (where there are more data sectors per rotation) and lower toward the inner tracks (where there are fewer data sectors per rotation); and is generally somewhat higher for 10,000-rpm drives. A current, widely used standard for the "buffer-to-computer" interface is 3.0 Gbit/s SATA, which can send about 300 megabyte/s (10-bit encoding) from the buffer to the computer, and thus is still comfortably ahead of today's^(\[*[as of?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Dates_and_numbers#Chronological_items "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Dates and numbers")*\]) disk-to-buffer transfer rates. Data transfer rate (read/write) can be measured by writing a large file to disk using special file-generator tools, then reading back the file. Transfer rate can be influenced by [file system fragmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File_system_fragmentation "File system fragmentation") and the layout of the files.^([\[132\]](#cite_note-itworld-2001-04-18-149))

HDD data transfer rate depends upon the rotational speed of the platters and the data recording density. Because heat and vibration limit rotational speed, advancing density becomes the main method to improve sequential transfer rates. Higher speeds require a more powerful spindle motor, which creates more heat. While areal density advances by increasing both the number of tracks across the disk and the number of sectors per track,^([\[135\]](#cite_note-153)) only the latter increases the data transfer rate for a given rpm. Since data transfer rate performance tracks only one of the two components of areal density, its performance improves at a lower rate.^([\[136\]](#cite_note-154))

## Access and interfaces

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Access and interfaces")\]

Main article: [Hard disk drive interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_drive_interface "Hard disk drive interface")

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/38/Seagate_ST33232A_hard_disk_inner_view.jpg/250px-Seagate_ST33232A_hard_disk_inner_view.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Seagate_ST33232A_hard_disk_inner_view.jpg)

Inner view of a 1998 [Seagate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seagate_Technology "Seagate Technology") HDD that used the [Parallel ATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_ATA "Parallel ATA") interface

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/89/2.5-inch_SATA_drive_on_top_of_a_3.5-inch_SATA_drive%2C_close-up_of_data_and_power_connectors.jpg/250px-2.5-inch_SATA_drive_on_top_of_a_3.5-inch_SATA_drive%2C_close-up_of_data_and_power_connectors.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:2.5-inch_SATA_drive_on_top_of_a_3.5-inch_SATA_drive,_close-up_of_data_and_power_connectors.jpg)

2.5-inch SATA drive on top of 3.5-inch SATA drive, showing close-up of (7-pin) data and (15-pin) power connectors

Current hard drives connect to a computer over one of several [bus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bus_(computing) "Bus (computing)") types, including parallel [ATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Technology_Attachment "Advanced Technology Attachment"), [Serial ATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_ATA "Serial ATA"), [SCSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SCSI "SCSI"), [Serial Attached SCSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_Attached_SCSI "Serial Attached SCSI") (SAS), and [Fibre Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibre_Channel "Fibre Channel"). Some drives, especially external portable drives, use [IEEE 1394](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_1394_interface "IEEE 1394 interface"), or [USB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USB "USB"). All of these interfaces are digital; electronics on the drive process the analog signals from the read/write heads. Current drives present a consistent interface to the rest of the computer, independent of the data encoding scheme used internally, and independent of the physical number of disks and heads within the drive.

Typically, a [DSP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signal_processor "Digital signal processor") in the electronics inside the drive takes the raw analog voltages from the read head and uses [PRML](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PRML "PRML") and [Reed–Solomon error correction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reed–Solomon_error_correction "Reed–Solomon error correction")^([\[137\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-79-155)) to decode the data, then sends that data out the standard interface. That DSP also watches the error rate detected by [error detection and correction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_detection_and_correction "Error detection and correction"), and performs [bad sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_sector "Bad sector") remapping, data collection for [Self-Monitoring, Analysis, and Reporting Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-Monitoring,_Analysis,_and_Reporting_Technology "Self-Monitoring, Analysis, and Reporting Technology"), and other internal tasks.

Modern interfaces connect the drive to the host interface with a single data/control cable. Each drive also has an additional power cable, usually direct to the power supply unit. Older interfaces had separate cables for data signals and for drive control signals.

- [Small Computer System Interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_Computer_System_Interface "Small Computer System Interface") (SCSI), originally named SASI for Shugart Associates System Interface, was standard on servers, workstations, [Commodore Amiga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amiga_peripherals "Amiga peripherals"), [Atari ST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atari_ST "Atari ST") and [Apple Macintosh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Macintosh "Apple Macintosh") computers through the mid-1990s, by which time most models had been transitioned to newer interfaces. The length limit of the data cable allows for external SCSI devices. The SCSI command set is still used in the more modern [SAS interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_Attached_SCSI "Serial Attached SCSI").
- [Integrated Drive Electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_Drive_Electronics "Integrated Drive Electronics") (IDE), later standardized under the name [AT Attachment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AT_Attachment "AT Attachment") (ATA, with the alias PATA ([Parallel ATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_ATA "Parallel ATA")) retroactively added upon introduction of SATA) moved the HDD controller from the interface card to the disk drive. This helped to standardize the host/controller interface, reduce the programming complexity in the host device driver, and reduced system cost and complexity. The 40-pin IDE/ATA connection transfers 16 bits of data at a time on the data cable. The data cable was originally 40-conductor, but later higher speed requirements led to an ["ultra DMA" (UDMA)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AT_Attachment#Interface "AT Attachment") mode using an 80-conductor cable with additional wires to reduce [crosstalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crosstalk "Crosstalk") at high speed.
- EIDE was an unofficial update (by Western Digital) to the original IDE standard, with the key improvement being the use of [direct memory access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_memory_access "Direct memory access") (DMA) to transfer data between the disk and the computer without the involvement of the [CPU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CPU "CPU"), an improvement later adopted by the official ATA standards. By directly transferring data between memory and disk, DMA eliminates the need for the CPU to copy byte per byte, therefore allowing it to process other tasks while the data transfer occurs.
- [Fibre Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibre_Channel "Fibre Channel") (FC) is a successor to parallel SCSI interface on enterprise market. It is a serial protocol. In disk drives usually the [Fibre Channel Arbitrated Loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibre_Channel_Arbitrated_Loop "Fibre Channel Arbitrated Loop") (FC-AL) connection topology is used. FC has much broader usage than mere disk interfaces, and it is the cornerstone of [storage area networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storage_area_network "Storage area network") (SANs). Recently other protocols for this field, like [iSCSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISCSI "ISCSI") and [ATA over Ethernet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATA_over_Ethernet "ATA over Ethernet") have been developed as well. Confusingly, drives usually use *copper* twisted-pair cables for Fibre Channel, not fiber optics. The latter are traditionally reserved for larger devices, such as servers or [disk array controllers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_array_controller "Disk array controller").
- [Serial Attached SCSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_Attached_SCSI "Serial Attached SCSI") (SAS). The SAS is a new generation serial communication protocol for devices designed to allow for much higher speed data transfers and is compatible with SATA. SAS uses a mechanically compatible data and power connector to standard 3.5-inch SATA1/SATA2 HDDs, and many server-oriented SAS RAID controllers are also capable of addressing SATA HDDs. SAS uses serial communication instead of the parallel method found in traditional SCSI devices but still uses SCSI commands.
- [Serial ATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_ATA "Serial ATA") (SATA). The SATA data cable has one data pair for differential transmission of data to the device, and one pair for differential receiving from the device, just like [EIA-422](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EIA-422 "EIA-422"). That requires that data be transmitted serially. A similar [differential signaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_signaling "Differential signaling") system is used in [RS485](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RS485 "RS485"), [LocalTalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LocalTalk "LocalTalk"), [USB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USB "USB"), [FireWire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FireWire "FireWire"), and differential [SCSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SCSI "SCSI"). SATA I to III are designed to be compatible with, and use, a subset of SAS commands, and compatible interfaces. Therefore, a SATA hard drive can be connected to and controlled by a SAS hard drive controller (with some minor exceptions such as drives/controllers with limited compatibility). However, they cannot be connected the other way round—a SATA controller cannot be connected to a SAS drive.

## Integrity and failure

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: Integrity and failure")\]

Main articles: [Hard disk drive failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_drive_failure "Hard disk drive failure"), [Head crash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_crash "Head crash"), and [Data recovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_recovery "Data recovery")

Due to the extremely close spacing between the heads and the disk surface, HDDs are vulnerable to being damaged by a [head crash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_crash "Head crash") – a [failure of the disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_failure "Hard disk failure") in which the head scrapes across the platter surface, often grinding away the thin magnetic film and causing data loss. Head crashes can be caused by electronic failure, a sudden power failure, physical shock, contamination of the drive's internal enclosure, wear and tear, [corrosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrosion "Corrosion"), or poorly manufactured platters and heads.

The HDD's spindle system relies on [air density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_density "Air density") inside the [disk enclosure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_enclosure "Disk enclosure") to support the heads at their proper [flying height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flying_height "Flying height") while the disk rotates. HDDs require a certain range of air densities to operate properly. The connection to the external environment and density occurs through a small hole in the enclosure (about 0.5 mm in breadth), usually with a filter on the inside (the *breather filter*).^([\[138\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-80-156)) If the air density is too low, then there is not enough lift for the flying head, so the head gets too close to the disk, and there is a risk of head crashes and data loss. Specially manufactured sealed and pressurized disks are needed for reliable high-altitude operation, above about 3,000 m (9,800 ft).^([\[139\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-81-157)) Modern disks include temperature sensors and adjust their operation to the operating environment. Breather holes can be seen on most disk drives, excluding sealed drives, such as drives that use helium, where any exposure to outside air would cause a failure – they usually have a sticker next to them, warning the user not to cover the holes. The air inside the operating drive is constantly moving too, being swept in motion by friction with the spinning platters. This air passes through an internal recirculation filter to remove any leftover contaminants from manufacture, any particles or chemicals that may have somehow entered the enclosure, and any particles or outgassing generated internally in normal operation. Very high humidity present for extended periods of time can corrode the heads and platters. An exception to this are hermetically sealed, helium-filled HDDs that largely eliminate environmental issues that can arise due to humidity or atmospheric pressure changes. Such HDDs were introduced by HGST in their first successful high-volume implementation in 2013.

For [giant magnetoresistive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant_magnetoresistive "Giant magnetoresistive") (GMR) heads in particular, a minor head crash from contamination (that does not remove the magnetic surface of the disk) still results in the head temporarily overheating, due to friction with the disk surface and can render the data unreadable for a short period until the head temperature stabilizes (so-called "thermal asperity", a problem which can partially be dealt with by proper electronic filtering of the read signal).

When the logic board of a hard disk fails, the drive can often be restored to functioning order and the data recovered by replacing the circuit board with one of an identical hard disk. In the case of read-write head faults, they can be replaced using specialized tools in a dust-free environment. If the disk platters are undamaged, they can be transferred into an identical enclosure and the data can be copied or cloned onto a new drive. In the event of disk-platter failures, disassembly and imaging of the disk platters may be required.^([\[140\]](#cite_note-158)) For logical damage to file systems, a variety of tools, including [fsck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fsck "Fsck") on [UNIX-like](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNIX-like "UNIX-like") systems and [CHKDSK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CHKDSK "CHKDSK") on [Windows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows "Windows"), can be used for [data recovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_recovery "Data recovery"). Recovery from logical damage can require [file carving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File_carving "File carving").

A common expectation is that hard disk drives designed and marketed for server use will fail less frequently than consumer-grade drives usually used in desktop computers. However, two independent studies by [Carnegie Mellon University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnegie_Mellon_University "Carnegie Mellon University")^([\[141\]](#cite_note-CMUDiskFailure-159)) and [Google](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google "Google")^([\[142\]](#cite_note-GoogleDiskFailure-160)) found that the "grade" of a drive does not relate to the drive's failure rate.

A 2011 summary of research, into SSD and magnetic disk failure patterns by [Tom's Hardware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom's_Hardware "Tom's Hardware") summarized research findings as follows:^([\[143\]](#cite_note-161))

- [Mean time between failures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_time_between_failures "Mean time between failures") (MTBF) does not indicate reliability; the [annualized failure rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annualized_failure_rate "Annualized failure rate") (AFR) is higher and usually more relevant.
- HDDs do not tend to fail during early use, and temperature has only a minor effect; instead, failure rates steadily increase with age.
- S.M.A.R.T. warns of mechanical issues but not other issues affecting reliability, and is therefore not a reliable indicator of condition.^([\[144\]](#cite_note-162))
- Failure rates of drives sold as "enterprise" and "consumer" are "very much similar", although these drive types are customized for their different operating environments.^([\[145\]](#cite_note-163)[\[146\]](#cite_note-164))
- In drive arrays, one drive's failure significantly increases the short-term risk of a second drive failing.

As of 2019^([\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit)), Backblaze, a storage provider, reported an annualized failure rate of two percent per year for a storage farm with 110,000 off-the-shelf HDDs with the reliability varying widely between models and manufacturers.^([\[147\]](#cite_note-Backblaze_3Q2019-165)) Backblaze subsequently reported in 2021 that the failure rate for HDDs and SSD of equivalent age was similar.^([\[7\]](#cite_note-BBRel2021-9)) An independent 2026 peer-reviewed analysis of the Backblaze data found that manufacturer differences remained after adjusting for drive age, capacity, form factor, and temperature.^([\[148\]](#cite_note-166))

To minimize cost and overcome failures of individual HDDs, storage systems providers rely on [redundant HDD arrays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAID "RAID"). HDDs that fail are replaced on an ongoing basis.^([\[147\]](#cite_note-Backblaze_3Q2019-165)[\[93\]](#cite_note-HAMR_2008_for_2009-108))

## Market segments

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section: Market segments")\]

### Consumer segment

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=23 "Edit section: Consumer segment")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/38/Two_Western_Digital_VelociRaptor_1_TB_SATA_10%2C000_rpm_3.5-inch_HDDs.jpg/250px-Two_Western_Digital_VelociRaptor_1_TB_SATA_10%2C000_rpm_3.5-inch_HDDs.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Two_Western_Digital_VelociRaptor_1_TB_SATA_10,000_rpm_3.5-inch_HDDs.jpg)

Two high-end consumer SATA 2.5-inch 10,000 rpm HDDs, factory-mounted in 3.5-inch adapter frames

#### Desktop HDDs

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=24 "Edit section: Desktop HDDs")\]

Desktop HDDs typically have one to five internal platters, rotate at 5,400 to 10,000 rpm, and have a media transfer rate of 0.5 Gbit/s or higher (1 GB = 10⁹ bytes; 1 Gbit/s = 10⁹ bit/s). Earlier (1980–1990s) drives tend to be slower in rotation speed. As of January 2025^([\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit)), the highest-capacity [desktop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desktop_computer "Desktop computer") HDDs stored 36[TB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terabyte "Terabyte"),^([\[149\]](#cite_note-167)[\[150\]](#cite_note-168)) with plans to release 50 TB drives later in 2025.^([\[151\]](#cite_note-169)) 36 TB HDDs were released in 2025.^(\[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*\]). As of 2016^([\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit)), the typical speed of a hard drive in an average desktop computer is 7,200 rpm, whereas low-cost desktop computers may use 5,900 rpm or 5,400 rpm drives. For some time in the 2000s and early 2010s some desktop users and data centers also used 10,000 rpm drives such as [Western Digital Raptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Digital_Raptor "Western Digital Raptor") but such drives have become much rarer as of 2016^([\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit)) (since the [WD VelociRaptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Digital_Raptor "Western Digital Raptor") was discontinued) and are not commonly used now, having been replaced by NAND flash-based SSDs.

#### Mobile (laptop) HDDs

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=25 "Edit section: Mobile (laptop) HDDs")\]

Smaller than their desktop and enterprise counterparts, they tend to be slower and have lower capacity, because typically has one internal platter and were 2.5" or 1.8" physical size instead of more common for desktops 3.5" form-factor. Mobile HDDs spin at 4,200 rpm, 5,200 rpm, 5,400 rpm, or 7,200 rpm, with 5,400 rpm being the most common; 7,200 rpm drives tend to be more expensive and have smaller capacities, while 4,200 rpm models usually were in older laptops and portables but are now outdated. Because of smaller platter(s), mobile HDDs generally have lower capacity than their desktop counterparts.

#### Consumer electronics HDDs

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=26 "Edit section: Consumer electronics HDDs")\]

These drives typically spin at 5400 rpm and include:

- **Video hard drives**, sometimes called "**surveillance hard drives**", are embedded into [digital video recorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_video_recorder "Digital video recorder") and provide a guaranteed streaming capacity, even in the face of read and write errors.^([\[152\]](#cite_note-VHD-170))
- Drives embedded into [automotive vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automotive_vehicle "Automotive vehicle"); they are typically built to resist larger amounts of shock and operate over a larger temperature range.

#### External and portable HDDs

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=27 "Edit section: External and portable HDDs")\]

See also: [USB mass storage device class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USB_mass_storage_device_class "USB mass storage device class") and [Disk enclosure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_enclosure "Disk enclosure")

Current external hard disk drives typically connect via [USB-C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USB-C "USB-C"); earlier models use USB-B (sometimes with using of a pair of ports for better bandwidth) or (rarely) [eSATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESATA "ESATA") connection. Variants using USB 2.0 interface generally have slower data transfer rates when compared to internally mounted hard drives connected through SATA. [Plug and play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plug_and_play "Plug and play") drive functionality offers system compatibility and features large storage options and portable design. As of March 2015^([\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit)), available capacities for external hard disk drives ranged from 500 GB to 10 TB.^([\[153\]](#cite_note-171)) External hard disk drives are usually available as assembled integrated products, but may be also assembled by combining an external [enclosure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_enclosure "Disk enclosure") (with USB or other interface) with a separately purchased drive. They are available in 2.5-inch and 3.5-inch sizes; 2.5-inch variants are typically called *portable external drives*, while 3.5-inch variants are referred to as *desktop external drives*. "Portable" drives are packaged in smaller and lighter enclosures than the "desktop" drives; additionally, "portable" drives use power provided by the USB connection, while "desktop" drives require external [power bricks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_brick "Power brick"). Features such as [encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self_encrypting_drive "Self encrypting drive"), [Wi-Fi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wi-Fi "Wi-Fi") connectivity,^([\[154\]](#cite_note-172)) biometric security or multiple interfaces (for example, [FireWire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FireWire "FireWire")) are available at a higher cost.^([\[155\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-98-173)) There are pre-assembled external hard disk drives that, when taken out from their enclosures, cannot be used internally in a laptop or desktop computer due to embedded USB interface on their [printed circuit boards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printed_circuit_board "Printed circuit board"), and lack of SATA (or [Parallel ATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_ATA "Parallel ATA")) interfaces.^([\[156\]](#cite_note-174)[\[157\]](#cite_note-175))

### Enterprise and business segment

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=28 "Edit section: Enterprise and business segment")\]

Server and workstation HDDs  
[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/cb/IBM_TotalStorage_Exp400.jpg/250px-IBM_TotalStorage_Exp400.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:IBM_TotalStorage_Exp400.jpg)
[Hot-swappable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot-swapping "Hot-swapping") HDD enclosure

Typically used with multiple-user computers running [enterprise software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enterprise_software "Enterprise software"). Examples are: transaction processing databases, internet infrastructure (email, webserver, e-commerce), scientific computing software, and nearline storage management software. Enterprise drives commonly operate continuously ("24/7") in demanding environments while delivering the highest possible performance without sacrificing reliability. Maximum capacity is not the primary goal, and as a result the drives are often offered in capacities that are relatively low in relation to their cost.^([\[158\]](#cite_note-AutoMK-99-176))

The fastest enterprise HDDs spin at 10,000 or 15,000 rpm, and can achieve sequential media transfer speeds above 1.6 Gbit/s^([\[159\]](#cite_note-erwpnw-177)) and a sustained transfer rate up to 1 Gbit/s.^([\[159\]](#cite_note-erwpnw-177)) Drives running at 10,000 or 15,000 rpm use smaller platters to mitigate increased power requirements (as they have less [air drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_drag "Air drag")) and therefore generally have lower capacity than the highest capacity desktop drives. Enterprise HDDs are commonly connected through [Serial Attached SCSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_Attached_SCSI "Serial Attached SCSI") (SAS) or [Fibre Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibre_Channel "Fibre Channel") (FC). Some support multiple ports, so they can be connected to a redundant [host bus adapter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host_bus_adapter "Host bus adapter").

Enterprise HDDs can have sector sizes larger than 512 bytes (often 520, 524, 528 or 536 bytes). The additional per-sector space can be used by hardware RAID controllers or applications for storing [Data Integrity Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_Integrity_Field "Data Integrity Field") (DIF) or Data Integrity Extensions (DIX) data, resulting in higher reliability and prevention of [silent data corruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silent_data_corruption "Silent data corruption").^([\[160\]](#cite_note-178))

## Economy

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=29 "Edit section: Economy")\]

### Price evolution

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=30 "Edit section: Price evolution")\]

HDD price per byte decreased at the rate of 40% per year during 1988–1996, 51% per year during 1996–2003 and 34% per year during 2003–2010.^([\[161\]](#cite_note-jcmit-179)[\[78\]](#cite_note-Byrne2015b-93)) The price decrease slowed down to 13% per year during 2011–2014, as areal density increase slowed and the [2011 Thailand floods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Thailand_floods "2011 Thailand floods") damaged manufacturing facilities^([\[83\]](#cite_note-Mellor_2014-11-10-98)) and have held at 11% per year during 2010–2017.^([\[162\]](#cite_note-180))

The [Federal Reserve Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Board "Federal Reserve Board") has published a quality-adjusted [price index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_index "Price index") for large-scale enterprise storage systems including three or more enterprise HDDs and associated controllers, racks and cables. Prices for these large-scale storage systems decreased at the rate of 30% per year during 2004–2009 and 22% per year during 2009–2014.^([\[78\]](#cite_note-Byrne2015b-93))

### Manufacturers and sales

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=31 "Edit section: Manufacturers and sales")\]

See also: [History of hard disk drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_hard_disk_drives "History of hard disk drives") and [List of defunct hard disk manufacturers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_defunct_hard_disk_manufacturers "List of defunct hard disk manufacturers")

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/87/Diagram_of_Hard_Disk_Drive_Manufacturer_Consolidation.svg/250px-Diagram_of_Hard_Disk_Drive_Manufacturer_Consolidation.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Diagram_of_Hard_Disk_Drive_Manufacturer_Consolidation.svg)

Diagram of HDD manufacturer consolidation

More than 200 companies have manufactured HDDs over time, but consolidations have concentrated production to just three manufacturers today: [Western Digital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Digital "Western Digital"), [Seagate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seagate_Technology "Seagate Technology"), and [Toshiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toshiba "Toshiba"). Production is mainly in the Pacific rim.

HDD unit shipments peaked at 651 million units in 2010 and have been declining since then to 166 million units in 2022.^([\[163\]](#cite_note-181)) Seagate at 43% of units had the largest market share.^([\[164\]](#cite_note-182))

## Competition from SSDs

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit&section=32 "Edit section: Competition from SSDs")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/dc/SEAGATE_7200.9_160GB_HDD%2B2230_256GB_NVME_SSD%2BSANDISK_X400_256GB_SSD.jpg/250px-SEAGATE_7200.9_160GB_HDD%2B2230_256GB_NVME_SSD%2BSANDISK_X400_256GB_SSD.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:SEAGATE_7200.9_160GB_HDD+2230_256GB_NVME_SSD+SANDISK_X400_256GB_SSD.jpg)

HDD and SSD

HDDs are being superseded by [solid-state drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid-state_drive "Solid-state drive") (SSDs) in markets where the higher speed (up to 7 [gigabytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gigabyte "Gigabyte") per second for [M.2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M.2 "M.2") (NGFF) [NVMe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NVMe "NVMe") drives^([\[165\]](#cite_note-183)) and 2.5 [gigabytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gigabyte "Gigabyte") per second for [PCIe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PCIe "PCIe") expansion card drives)^([\[166\]](#cite_note-184))), ruggedness, and lower power of SSDs are more important than price, since the bit cost of SSDs is four to nine times higher than HDDs.^([\[16\]](#cite_note-blocks_August2019-18)[\[15\]](#cite_note-jcmit_2019-17)) As of 2016^([\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit)), HDDs are reported to have a failure rate of 2–9% per year, while SSDs have fewer failures: 1–3% per year.^([\[167\]](#cite_note-Google_2016-185)) However, SSDs have more un-correctable data errors than HDDs.^([\[167\]](#cite_note-Google_2016-185))

SSDs are available in larger capacities (up to 100 TB)^([\[40\]](#cite_note-100TB_2018-53)) than the largest HDD, as well as higher storage densities (100 TB and 30 TB SSDs are housed in 2.5 inch HDD cases with the same height as a 3.5-inch HDD),^([\[168\]](#cite_note-186)[\[169\]](#cite_note-187)[\[170\]](#cite_note-188)[\[171\]](#cite_note-189)[\[172\]](#cite_note-190)) although such large SSDs are very expensive.

A laboratory demonstration of a 1.33 Tb 3D NAND chip with 96 layers (NAND commonly used in [solid-state drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid-state_drive "Solid-state drive") (SSDs)) had 5.5 Tbit/in² as of 2019^([\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hard_disk_drive&action=edit))),^([\[173\]](#cite_note-EETimes_2019Feb-191)) while the maximum areal density for HDDs is 1.5 Tbit/in². The areal density of flash memory is doubling every two years, similar to [Moore's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore's_law "Moore's law") (40% per year) and faster than the 10–20% per year for HDDs. In 2025, the maximum capacity was 36 terabytes for a HDD,^([\[174\]](#cite_note-Mearian_2015Dec-192)) and 100 terabytes for an SSD.^([\[175\]](#cite_note-Coughlin_2016Feb-193)) HDDs were used in 70% of the desktop and notebook computers produced in 2016, and SSDs were used in 30%.^([\[176\]](#cite_note-Coughlin_2016June-194)) In 2025 HDDs are not found in laptops, if not very rarely, and most desktops come with an SSD only, though some still are configured with an SSD and HDD, or rarely with an HDD only.^(\[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*\])

The market for silicon-based flash memory (NAND) chips, used in SSDs and other applications, is growing faster than for HDDs. Worldwide NAND revenue grew 16% per year from \$22 billion to \$57 billion during 2011–2017, while production grew 45% per year from 19 exabytes to 175 exabytes.^([\[177\]](#cite_note-IBM_Fontana_2018-195))
