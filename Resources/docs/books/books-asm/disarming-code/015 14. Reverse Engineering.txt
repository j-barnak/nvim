
Reverse Engineering

     Reverse Engineering could have easily merited an entire book, and even that
wouldn't adequately suffice, since so many approaches vary on a case by case basis.
This lone chapter does not even purport to be comprehensive. Even if it were, reversing
is a skill which takes many years to master.

    Each binary brings with it its own challenges, and some necessitate different
approaches: A top-down approach (starting at the main and tracing the flow), or a
bottom up one. Many workflows are possible, but all share similar stages. This chapter
suggests one possible workflow. First, in theory, then with some specific case studies
that illustrate its stages. The idea is not to tackle the binary as a whole, but to break it
into "byte size " chunks.

          Reverse Engineering existed before engineering itself came to be . Since time
      immemorial, humans sought to take apart constructs - first, those of nature, then those
      of others - in order to figure out what makes them work, and how to learn from them.

          This approach is prevalent when it comes to computer code, as well. Most binaries
      are closed source, and there is tremendous value in taking them apart, and figuring out
      the code behind them . Whether it's for figuring out what the competition does, or to
      find potential vulnerabilities, reverse engineering proves a useful skill to have.

          Even in cases where commercial binaries derive, either in part or fully, from open
      sources, there is value in statically and dynamically analyzing a given binary, and
      mapping its functions to those in the source. This is important when trying to locate a
      particular code path, perhaps a vulnerability, or to see how the compiled binary deviates
      from given open sources.

          No work could possibly allege to provide all the necessary know-how to become a
      master reverse engineer. As with all skills, "reversing" is acquired and honed through
      practice, and much time. The techniques used, and their efficacy, can also greatly vary
      depending on the software subjected to analysis. Tools such as Ghidra and IDA make
      the job immensely easier by offering decompilers - but true decompilation to clearly
      readable source (minus comments) still remains outside contemporary capabilities.

           With the above in mind, this chapter aims to provide a gentle but thorough
      introduction to this fine art. We present a methodology - by no means the only one, yet
      nonetheless an efficient workflow - which can be used in most cases to slowly but surely
      reverse a binary. The high level idea is to perform a few simple steps - identifying
      dependencies, pointers, and functions - before iterating into each function and code
      block thus identified, and throughly analyzing them to the point where their
      functionality is understood.

          Note, that while most analysis will start at the binary's entry pOint, this does not
      imply a top down approach is always best. In fact, a bottom up approach, starting at
      the leaf functions, often proves easier. Those functions which call no others (or, in some
      cases, only call well known external dependencies), can then be abstracted as APIs,
      which in turn simplify their callers, at which point the process may repeat .

1.2. Ident ifying Pointers in Data

     The data segment of a binary will often contain pOinters, to be loaded at runtime
into variables and dereferenced by code. Such pOinters must always * be ali gned (that
is, in the 64-bit case, the address of the pOinter must end in '0' or '8'). De pendi ng on
the binary type, these may stand out immediately, require further process ing, or
(rarely) suggest ambiguity.

   •   In the case of kernel-space binaries, the pOinters tend to "bounce off the hex", as
       the 0xFFFFFFF ... is clearly visible in the last three bytes of every eight byte
       group being 'FF'.

* - Unless obfuscation is employed, at the cost of a minor performance impediment, since loading an
unaligned pointer involves more instructions.

         •   In the case of user-s pace binari es, paths diverge:
                o   Addresses in Mach-O binari es fa ll in the 0x1 ....••. , range . Wh en put to
                    individua l bytes, this will set the last three bytes of every eight byte group
                    to '00', and the one preceding them to '01'. The first four bytes of every
                    byte group wi ll appear to be a low 32-bit quantity. The caveat here is that
                    false positives of the form of two 32 - bit quantities, whose second quantity
                    is a 'I', might rarely be encountered.
                o   Addresses in ELF/PE are fully relocatable, so pOinters in user-space binaries
                    will actually be in the 16-24 least significant bits (i.e. first 2-3 bytes ) of
                    eve ry eight byte group, with the remaining bytes all '00'. There are
                    naturally more ambiguities here, as it cou ld be hard sometimes to tell a 64-
                    bit pOinter from two 32-bit qua ntiti es, with th e latter being all '00'.

          Thankfully, ambiguiti es can automatically be resolved by the linker. Recall (from
      Chapter 3), that all binary formats support position independent executables, and
      therefore must encode pointers in data, in the form of relocation opcodes (PE/ELF) or
      fixup chains (Mach-O) . This means that most disassmblers will automatically identify
      and display pOinters in data when dumping.

      1.2.1. Pointers to Text (code)

          Pointers to text are function pointers, which are common ly found in object oriented
      code (as the "m ethod s" of object instances or classes) , RPC dispatch tabl es, and
      otherwise . In the ARM64 architectures, such pOinte rs must be aligned on a 4-byte
      boundary, so the ir last digit will be '0', '4', '8' or 'C'. Another indicator of such pointers is
      that they usually point to a function prolog instructions (commonly, STP, SUB of SP,
      PACI [AB] SP or BTl c), unless the function is optimized as a (e.g. a fastcall).

      1.2.2. Pointers to Data

           Pointe rs to data are pOinters to strings, structures, or in some cases other pOinters.
      The string variety is most com mon , for example in constant-to-string lookup tables.
      String pointers are also the easiest to recognize, since the strings pOinted to are often
      human readabl e. Stri ng pointers need not be aligned, but will usually fall in a rio data
      se gment (PE/ELF) or TEXT._cstring section (Mach-O) . Other data pointers are
      alig ned according to the data pOinted to, commo nly 8-byte.

      1.3. Identifying functions and code blocks
           Code is compri sed of code blocks and functions, and barring rare exceptions
      (n otably, inlining), functions can be easily detected in a binary. There are three common
      ways to detect fu nctions in code:

         •   Exp-orted functions: will be clearly visible in the binary's symbol table -
             es pecially in .so/ .dylib/. dll. Since the point of exporting is to make them available ,
             they ca nnot be stripped, and provide the API entry pOints into the binary.
         •   Left over symbols or debug data: can reveal some or all of the functions in a
             file . Mach-O files ' LCJUNCTlON_STARTS is particularly useful for this, as was
             discussed in Chapter 3.
         •   Disassembly of the text sections: is the best way to reveal all functions. This
             can generally be done by one of two methods:
               1. Identifying susp-ect p-rologs: As shown in Chapter 6, function prologs
                  autogenerated by the compiler have a distinctly recognizable structure. In
                  PAC binaries, the presence of a PACI [AB] SP instruction practically
                  guarantees it is the beg inning of a functio n.
              2.    Locating branches in code: Explicit ca lls (BL and BLRs. Less com monly,
                    unconditional branches to other code blocks at the end of a fun ction imply a
                    tail ca ll. Following BL [R] has the advantage of locating prolog - less
                    functions .

           During the process of figuring out the functions, some identified functions or code
      blocks may seem to be unreachable from other parts of the code. Finding potentially
      "d ead code" is not uncommon, but requires verification. Oftentimes the code block is
      not reachable from within the text segment by a direct branch, but may nonethe less be
      invocable through a text pOinter in data. This is quite often the case in object oriented
      code, wherein "object methods" are a catchier name for function pOinters. Another
      common case of invoking function pointers is in the code behind Darwin' s blocks,
      discussed later.

more (e.g . switch)). These constructs are not only easy to detect, but also important,

1.3.2. References and Cross-References

    One way to look at a code block is to envision it as a tree . The root is the entry
pOint fun ction , and its branches made up (recursively) by calls to other functions, until
the very leaf functions, which call no others . To somewhat help with the depth of the
tree, calls made by functions to known APls - system or library calls - need not to be
further traversed, which helps make more functions into leaves (and thus ending the
analysis earlier) . This is arguably an over-simpl ification (loops in code would imply
cycles in the tree), but it's a good enough approximation .

callers. The importance of this is obvious. More often than not, however, the opposite
fun ctionality - see which functions are called by a particular function - is just as useful,
if not more so.

1.4. Function Call Arguments
     A key part of reverse engineering is - aside from isolating function blocks - to also
det ermine the arguments supplied to them. A functional block can be thought of a
"b lack box" when its exact input and output flow can be determined. This can save
much time as such functions can be exempted from further analysis, and - recursively -
so can any functions called by them.

    In other words, think of any complex function, however complicated it may be.
Whatever it is, given the same arguments (and putting aside randomness in rare cases,
or external inputs, which are also rare), it will produce the same output. Though
symbols may be stripped, all functions had meaningful names before they were
removed .

    If possible , it's important to determin e the argument types early on. This can be
challenging, since at the assembly level arguments registers are commonly accessed as
eithe r Xn or Wn, which discloses 32- or 64-bitness, but doesn't mention the argument
type. This can be overcome in one of several ways:

   •   Runtime metadata: such as Swift's and particularly Objective-C's, will easily
       disclose the argument types through the function signature string. Objective-C
       will actually detail not just the types, but the argument names in the selector.
   •   Argument use: in particular, through *p rintf () or logging variants, will disclose
       the exact type, due the the format specifiers used.

     Once an argument type (and/or name) has been determined for a certain function,
it can help clarify further functions by tracing its flow both downstream and upstream.
This is because many arguments end up being passed around between functions.

      1.5. Identifying Variables and Structures

          In addition to their arguments, functions will often use variables. These may hold
      data derived from intermediary ca lculations on the arguments, child function calls, or
      external, global data.

      1.5.1. Local variables

           Loca l variab les are vital to make functions reentrant, and thus more usefu l. Since
      loca ls are set up on the stack frame, their cumulative size is often bounded by whatever
      th e function prolog set up as the frame size (i.e. the difference between the frame
      po inter, X29, and the stack pointer, SP. Commonly, the stack frame size can be deduced
      from the function prolog, though there are two notable exceptions to this :

         •   When the program is compiled with optimizations (-fomit-frame-pointer), X2 9
             might be left unused, and all references instead be to the stack pOinter.
         • The stack frame size may dynamically change during the function's lifetime. Th is
           may occur as a resu lt of in-b lock variable definitions, or calls to a lloca (3).

      1.5.2. Identifying Globals

           Globa l vari ab les are accessed f rom more than one sco pe , and must the refo re be
      initialized in t he binary' s data segme nt. Here, the base add ressi ng wi ll not be relative to

    1.5.3. Identifying Structures

        Structures are just a convenient way of assigning multiple variables into the same
    context group. At the assembly level, this translates into LDR* or STR* calls based at
    either some register value . That value is either one loaded from ADR/ADP (for global
    structures), or from SP/FP + offset.

        The hitch here is that, when referring to structure members, the offset will not
    necessarily be the same as the offsetof () the field in the structure. For local
    structures, it may be from the start of the frame. For global ones, it may be the start of
    the page containing multiple globals. In rare cases (such as when a structure is exactly
    at the top of the stack frame), the offsets may align neatly, but that is the exception,
    not the rule .

        When structures are passed as arguments, the pattern is just as clear, with the
    base being one of the X0, Xl, etc. argument registers . As you may recall, structures in C
    may be passed by value (essentially, as a copy of the original structure) or by
    reference (as in, the address of the original structure) . Irrespective of method, the
    assembly level implementation is always by reference.

    1.6. Symbolication

        Symbols are of little use outside of the debugging environment, so most code will
    be stripped prior to its release. Symbolicated copies (or companion files) for the release
    code can be set aside, so as to diagnose an d deal with any crash reports .

        From a reverse engineer's perspective, however, symbols are vital. A meaningful
    name for a functions provides a valuable hint as to its purpose. In the case of C++,
    Objective-C, Swift and other languages, the symbols may also mangle the argument
    names and/or datatypes. Symbolication - The process of reconstructing a given
    binary's symbols - is therefore an important skill to master.

        In the uncommon case, where the debug build or companion file of a given binary
    can be located, symbolication becomes trivial. Most disassemblers can recognize the
    symbols, and perform the symbol to address translation. Commonly, however, the
    symbols are kept separate from the retail builds , which may further be stripped, so as
    to remove all symbols but the ones absolutely vital for linking .

      1.8. Obfuscation

          Given such procedures for reverse engineering, it is obvious that countermeasures
      wo uld ex ist. Developers who wish to keep their Intellectual Property secret, whether for
      legitimate reason s or malicious ones, can employ obfuscation t ec hniques, in order to
      make their binari es hard to reverse.

      1.8.1. Code Encryption

          The id ea of being able to distribute a compl etely encrypted binary is tempting . Such
      a binary would be completely opaque to static analysis , and - given a secure platform -
      impervious to dynamic analysis as well.

           For an encrypted binary to even be possible, a "bring your own" (not platform -
      assisted) implementation would require some memory pages to be designated as rwx,
      since an unencrypted "bootstrapper" portion of the code would need to take the
      encrypted porti on, and decrypt it into executable memory. While this is possible in
      Lin ux, Darwin prevents such memory protecti o ns from coexisting on the same page,
      unl ess debugging or JIT entitlements are used . Those very same entitlements also
      exempt ad-hoc generated or self-modifying code fro m code signi ng requirements.

           Another option is to piggyback on an existing dynami c runtime environm ent -
      common ly, Javascript or Python, since those can take cod e, interpret it or JIT-compile it,
      and then execute it. In those cases, the code payload can be a long code expression,
      wh ich is delivered as part of an encrypted payl oad , decrypted in the target process'
      memory, and then evaluated .

           It's tempting to think of an encryption as a panacea, but its use is rather limited.
      For one, there is the chicken and egg problem of key manag eme nt, which is not at all
      trivial. A co mm on approach here is to provide the key during runtime, since a key
      embedded within the binary can be found in mu ch simpler ways.

           Even if the key management problem were some how solv ed , however, the
      processor at one point or another needs to execute the code and process the data, at
      wh ich point everything reverts to plaintext. A debug ger or emu lator would, at that
      point, be able to break or singl e step, and recover the logi c.

           Darwin, like many other novel features , has pioneered code encryption - First, with
      the move to the Intel architecture came the SG_PROTECTED_VERSION_l binari es . These
      were key platform binaries (Finder.app, mds and a few others), encrypted by a key
      stashed deep in the BIOS. The app le_p rotect pager would get access to the key
      (through the Dont Steal MacOS .kext), and decrypt them on the fly. Th e key was easy, not
      to say trivial to reverse (as expl ain ed in M-II/ll), but th is is where Apple cleverly put it
      into a short poem form - stating a copyrighted work and part of the key. This entitled
      Apple to lega l copyright protections, whi ch were in fact used successfu lly to prevent
      clo ning of Intel-based Ma cs.

          With the move to Appl e Silicon platforms - particu larly the *OS variants - a different
      form of code encryption was used. The LCENCRYPTION_INFO deSignates a portion of
      the binary - usually, its _ TEXT segment - as encryption with some en cryptio n type .
      Presently, only 0 ( unen crypted) and 1 (FairPlay) are supported .

          FairPlay, originally devised for iTu nes music download but shunned by consumers,
      has thus gained new life . Applications downloaded from the App Store are encrypted,
      and keyed by a strong symmetric key embedded in the App Store Receipt . This key is
      also en crypted, and set to decrypt on ly on i- Devices with a specific GID (device-class
      specific) key, optio nally coupled with the user's Apple ID.

      1.8.2. String Encryption

          Much was discussed on the importance of str ings in reverse engineering . When code
      cannot be easily obfuscated , strings make a "low hanging fruit" . There are two
      approaches here:

        •   Debugllog.g ing outp-ut: Can be obfuscated by hashing it. This is usually by a
            simple application of a keyed hash (e.g . MD5/SHA-l/ SHA- 256/e tc with a
            developer known key), using a static ma cro over the string messages, #def ined
            at co mpile time , but with no trace in the output assembly. When debu g/log ging
            output is return ed to the developer, it's a simple matter to perform a "known
            plaintext" on all messages, and figure out t he source of the otherwise cryptic
            hashes.

   •   Strings used as arguments: will usually be passed through some "decryptor"
       function first. The uusal practice here is to use the "key" embedded somewhere in
       the binary (or downloaded from the Internet, if possible), and then utilize plenty
       of bitwise operations (mainly, EOR). This, however, can be defeated by
       breakpointing at the inevitable point where the plaintext string is used with some
       platform API (for example, if the string is used for dlopen(3) or dlsym(3).

1.8.3. Code Obfuscation

     With code encryption proving difficult (and largely fruitless, outside Darwin), the
next best approach is code obfuscation. The idea here is to complicate static analysis by
unnecessary twists and ties which would make it harder to automatically follow register
values and function calls. The simple linear flow can be broken by numerous branches,
which makes it harder for disassemblers to follow the arguments in between code
chunks . The IIvm-obfuscator and hikari tools are commonly used here, as is (with very
limited results) Android's DEXguard and PROguard.

     Code obfuscation involves also masking (as much as possible), library and system
calls made by the code. Commonly, the imports are left untouched, but the calls
themselves are made via registers (i.e. BR/BLR, rather than B/BL. The register value is
obfuscated with one of the previously mentioned techniques - "encryption", flow-
breaks, loading from encrypted memory, etc. As another hurdle, dlopen(3)/dlsym(3)
are used heavily, to prevent static analysis of imports from having any idea of the
code's functionality.

    Platform binaries are commonly left unobfuscated. Curiously enough, in the Darwin
case, only those dealing with FairPlay (fairplayd.XX, lskdd, and the FairPlayIOKit.kext) are
heavily obfuscated.

game, however, since every countermeasure has a "counter-countermeasure":

      2.6. Blocks
          A detailed discussion of GCD ca n also be found in M-I/8 (with some unavoidable
      overlap). The view taken here is that of user mode APls, with a particular focus on
      reversing blocks.

           Way back in macOS 10.6, Apple introduced the Grand Central Dispatcher (GCD) as
      an alternative programming model to thread. Slowly but surely, GCD has managed to
      push out traditional pthread programming - first, in Appl e's own fram eworks and
      libraries, and then in the Darwin SDKs . POSIX threads are still supported - and
      occasionally used under the hood - but GCD enti rely obviates the need to use them
      directly, abstracting them nearly perfectly. Apple has since rewritten most of its code to
      use blocks, eliminating almost all pth read (3) API calls.

           GCD brings a paradigm shift: Instead of thinking in terms of thread-oriented
      programming, it offers block-oriented programming. A block is similar to the C/C++
      curly-braced block, with the notabl e exception that it self contains its state. This makes
      it easy for the developer to dispatch the block to execution, without getting into the
      thread management specifics - which is handled automatically by the platform .

      seen as instances of an NSObj ect descendant. There are two types* of blocks:

         •   The NSConcreteStackBlock is created dynamically, on the thread's stack .
         •   The NSConc reteGloba lBlo ck is created at compile-time, and placed in the

         2.7. Case Study: Darwin - MIG handlers

             Contemporary Darwin clients and servers use Apple 's XPC as their RPC mechanism
        of choice . XPC is the preferred mechanism, which passes typed data over lower level
        Ma ch messages . That said, a surprising amount of client-server interactions are still
        carried over th e legacy MIG, serialized using the Mach Interface Generator utility of the
        sam e name. These include quite a few daemons in j usrj libexec, as well as the kernel
        itself.

               Both MIG clients and servers contain auto-gene rated code produced by the mig
        util ity. The client code handles the requ est generation and reply processing end,
        commonly known as the "proxy" in RPC parlance. The server code, co mmonly known as

        proxy and stub is generated based on a .defs file, in whi ch request/reply IDs and APIs
        are specified .
